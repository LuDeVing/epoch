# Organisational ambidexterity: deep document

*Subfield E14.03 of innovation studies. Sources: evidence_a-d.jsonl and evidence_x.jsonl (ids a01 to d20, x01 to x06), claims.jsonl (K01 to K23), critique.md. Status marks: [E] established, [S] single-source, [C] contested. Read with SCHEMA.md (G1-G12, S0-S9, O1-O8, section 4b).*

**Honest summary up front.** This is a human-organisation literature. No paper tests it on LLM agents (a29, d20). The human evidence is mixed and mostly survey-based. The LLM evidence is about exploration versus exploitation in bandits and evolutionary search. It is an analogy, not a test. The best use for our agent is "background only", with a few cheap tests (section 8).

## 1. What it is

Organisational ambidexterity is the ability of an organisation to do two opposed things at once: exploit what it knows (efficiency, refinement, incremental innovation) and explore what it does not (search, experiment, radical innovation). March (1991) set the frame: pure exploration pays experiment costs without benefits, and pure exploitation traps a system in a stable but poor equilibrium (a21). Duncan (1976) coined "ambidextrous" (a10).

Three ways to get both:
- **Structural.** Separate units. One unit explores, one exploits. Senior leaders integrate them (Tushman and O'Reilly 1996; O'Reilly and Tushman 2007, a17).
- **Contextual.** Same unit, same people. The unit's context lets each person switch between alignment (do the job efficiently) and adaptability (change the job). Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004 (a01).
- **Sequential or temporal.** Alternate: exploit for a long time, then explore after a shock (Duncan 1976; Gupta, Smith and Shalley 2006, a26).

The open empirical questions: does balance pay, which form pays when, and what environment decides it (b11).

For our agent the mapping is loose. "Explore" is generating new candidate approaches (S3 in open mode, operators O1 to O8 at S6). "Exploit" is testing, revising and selecting (S4, S5, S7, S8). "Structural" would be separate explorer and exploiter agents. "Contextual" would be one agent that switches inside one loop. Our schema is already contextual and code-switched (G1, G12).

## 2. History and theories in depth

**March 1991 (a21, a30).** Exploration and exploitation compete for scarce resources. Exploitation gives sure, near returns. Exploration gives uncertain, distant returns. Systems drift toward exploitation because its feedback is fast. About 12,500 citations. Only one quote was opened here; March's model itself was not.

**Duncan 1976 and the sequential form.** Firms switch structures over time. Gupta et al. 2006 (a26, not opened) separate sequential from simultaneous ambidexterity and note they pose different management problems. The punctuated-equilibrium view: structural suits long stable periods broken by rare discontinuities (b11).

**Tushman and O'Reilly 1996-97, structural.** Separate subunits, each internally aligned and with its own culture, linked by shared strategic intent, common values, a senior team that integrates, and linking mechanisms (a17). Evidence cited by O'Reilly and Tushman 2007: 22 innovation attempts in 13 companies. Ambidextrous designs beat functional, cross-functional and spin-out designs. The exception was product substitution, where simpler designs won (a18). They found more than 40 ambidexterity studies at the time (a17). Both authors are proponents of the structural form.

**Gibson and Birkinshaw 2004, contextual.** Context has four traits: stretch and discipline (performance management), support and trust (social context). They are interdependent, not substitutable (a01, a07). Several paths reach ambidexterity (equifinality, a08). The authors are proponents of the contextual form.

**Jansen, Van den Bosch and Volberda 2006, antecedents and environment.** Formal and informal coordination, and how the environment decides which innovation pays (a13 to a16). Informal connectedness explains more than formal mechanisms (a16).

**He and Wong 2004, balance as fit.** Two tests: the interaction of explorative and exploitative strategies (positive for growth) and the relative imbalance (negative) (a11, x02). Introduced the "product" and "imbalance" measures.

**Cao, Gedajlovic and Zhang 2009, two dimensions.** Balance (equal levels) differs from combined magnitude (total level). Balance helps resource-poor firms. Combined magnitude helps resource-rich firms. Both high gives synergy (a22, x03).

**Uotila et al. 2009, objective data.** Using S&P 500 financials, the share of exploration has an inverted-U link to performance. The best share moves with industry R&D intensity (d06). Lubatkin et al. 2006 used 139 SMEs with multisource ratings and kept a positive link (d07).

**Meta-analysis era.** Junni 2013 (b04, d04), Mathias 2014 (x01, d05), Mathias, McKenny and Crook 2018 (b06), Fourne et al. 2019 (d01, d02), Wenke et al. 2021 (d03), Kula 2023 (b08). They disagree (section 5).

**Reviews.** Raisch and Birkinshaw 2008 organises the field into antecedents, moderators and outcomes (a24). Citation counts show influence: March 1991 12,505; Tushman and O'Reilly 1996 5,057; He and Wong 2004 between 2,934 and 4,489 depending on database (x02, a30; the earlier 777 is a single-database figure); Jansen 2006 3,842; Gupta 2006 3,421; Raisch and Birkinshaw 2,842; Lubatkin 2006 2,242; Cao 2009 1,503; Junni 2013 907.

**Counter-views.** Christensen: explorers must be spun out. Strategy work: firms pursuing both get "stuck in the middle". Ebben and Johnson 2005: 300 small companies; those pursuing both efficiency and flexibility did worse than focused firms (a19).

## 4. Mechanisms and models

Each model below is stated so it can be coded. Treat them as background mechanisms; human findings are not proved for LLMs.

**4.1 The ambidexterity score (what is being measured).**
- Product: E x X (He and Wong interaction). Rewards both high.
- Imbalance: |E - X|. He and Wong: negative for growth.
- Combined magnitude: E + X. Cao 2009: helps resource-rich firms.
- Balance: 1 - |E-X|/(E+X). Cao 2009: helps resource-poor firms.
- Junni 2013: the link is stronger with combined than balance measures (b04). So the choice of formula changes the result. For our agent, log explore tokens E and exploit tokens X per item and fit all four.

**4.2 Share model.** Let s = explore share of budget B. Uotila 2009 (d06) gives an inverted-U: performance P(s) with an optimum s* that rises with environmental novelty (R&D intensity). Implementation: sweep s in {0, .1, .25, .5, .75, 1} at fixed B per task class; fit a quadratic; store s* per class (S0 lookup, S9 update).

**4.3 Structural versus contextual as agent topologies.**
- Structural: agent X explores (S3, S6), agent Y exploits (S4, S5, S7, S8), a controller passes the best candidates. Cost: handoff tokens and lost shared context.
- Contextual: one agent in one context; code switches its mode by state, with different guardrails per mode (loose on generation, strict on test). This is our current design.
- Sequential: exploit until a signal (impasse, S6), then a bounded explore burst, then return.
Equal-token evidence favours the single agent (d14, d15). Human evidence says structural helps balance in high-tech, contextual helps services (d01, d02). The agent analogue of "sector" is "task type": separable stages versus interactive ones. Untested.

**4.4 Context traits as scaffold properties (Gibson and Birkinshaw).** Stretch (hard goals), discipline (clear rules and feedback), support (help), trust (permission to fail). Analogue for a scaffold: ambitious target on the generate step, strict code checks on the test step, a safe place to log failed attempts (memory of failures), no penalty for dropped candidates. This is a loose design analogy; the context-trait interaction (F=5.41) is a single-study human result (K04).

**4.5 Formal versus informal coordination (Jansen).** Formalisation helps exploitation (beta .18); centralisation hurts exploration (beta -.17); informal connectedness helps both (about .20). Analogue: tight rubric and fixed format at the exploit stage (verify, select); loose, non-central generation at the explore stage (fresh contexts, per G4 and S3); a shared, lightly structured note store as "connectedness". Single study (K07).

**4.6 Code-driven exploration controller (strongest implementable mechanism).** From MACE, Krishnamurthy and d09:
- Keep counts n_i and mean reward r_i per candidate strategy or peer, in code.
- Score_i = r_i + c * sqrt(ln(t) / n_i) (UCB), or Thompson sampling.
- The LLM only fills the slot: it executes the chosen strategy.
- Inferior-peer lock-in fell from 50.0% to 0.0% with external UCB in one small-model setting (c03). Giving the UCB formula in the prompt only reached 23.3%.
- An externally computed summary of history is what made GPT-4 explore (Krishnamurthy: only CoT plus external summary worked).
This matches G1 and G5 (code selects).

**4.7 Adaptive allocation (d10, d11).** Treat generation arms (operators, prompts, temperatures, model sizes) as bandit arms. Allocate each next call by Thompson or UCB. A pool of 5 to 20 arms was best; 50 dilutes refinement. Add a capability gate: a weak model gets no depth gain, so do not give it long refinement chains.

**4.8 Diversity control in the candidate archive.** Embedding-similarity novelty rejection (threshold 0.95 in ShinkaEvolve, c16); MAP-Elites archive over quality and a behaviour descriptor (QDAIF, c13; LoongFlow, c18); entropy-controlled Boltzmann selection: when population diversity falls, raise temperature (c18). Islands: independent subpopulations with rare migration (c17). Measured effect of the island-type controls is small (d12). Novelty rejection helps and an extra LLM novelty judge adds little (c16), consistent with G8.

**4.9 Generate versus select split (Harris and Slivkins).** The LLM proposes candidates from a large semantic space; a cheap statistical rule selects. LLMs lost to linear regression at exploitation but helped at exploration (c08, c10). Maps to S3 (LLM) and S7 (code).

## 5. What holds up

**Replications and failures of the founding claim.**
- Founding study (Gibson and Birkinshaw): 41 units, cross-sectional, perceived performance, one survey wave. The top-group advantage disappears with separate raters (4.11 vs 4.13; a05). No independent replication of the same design was found.
- Independent human studies disagree in direction. Positive: He and Wong 2004 (N=206, x02); Lubatkin 2006 (139 SMEs, multisource; d07); Chandrasekaran 2012 (34 high-tech firms, via Wikipedia only, b15). Negative: Ebben and Johnson (N=300) and Clauss 2020 (N=150) (a19, x04).
- Meta-analyses: Junni 2013 positive but conditional (b04); Mathias 2014 ambidexterity weaker than focus (x01); Wenke 2021 the same for SMEs (d03); Kula 2023 g=.77 (b24). These cannot be reconciled without moderator data. Kula is one author with limited databases and is probably inflated by common-source surveys. Effect sizes for Junni, Mathias, Fourne and Wenke were never read.

**Structural versus contextual head to head.** Only Fourne et al. 2019 tests it (d01, d02). Abstract only: structural separation helps balance at all sizes and more in high-tech; service firms benefit from avoiding separation. The outcome is balance, not performance, so it does not show that structural wins on results. The reviews seen here come from proponents on both sides (O'Reilly and Tushman; Gibson and Birkinshaw).

**Moderators that recur.**
- Environment: dynamism favours exploration, competitiveness favours exploitation (Jansen; a15). Low munificence: no benefit from balance (b10).
- Resources: balance for poor, magnitude for rich (Cao; x03). Small firms without slack may do better one-sided (b10, d03).
- Measurement: combined versus balance; perceptual versus objective; level of analysis; cross-sectional versus multimethod (b04).
- Firm age, long-term performance, dynamic measurement (b06).
- Market orientation: without it ambidextrous firms had lower financial performance (b10, Wikipedia-level).
- Sector: services gain from avoiding separation (d02).
- Property-rights protection weakens single-orientation links in SMEs (d03).

**Data-quality weak spots.** The same managers often rate both ambidexterity and performance, so correlations such as r=.78 are likely inflated (K10). Objective data gives a different shape (inverted-U, Uotila). Multisource design (Lubatkin) keeps a positive link but the coefficients were not read. Counts are inconsistent across databases (He and Wong 777 vs 2,934 vs 4,489; Gibson-Birkinshaw 4,234 vs 4,195 respondents).

**Interventions that worked or did not (human).**
- Worked in case and survey evidence: ambidextrous design for innovation attempts (22 attempts in 13 firms; a18), behavioural integration of the top team (a20), shared vision (211 banks; a20), informal connectedness (a14).
- Did not: centralisation (hurts exploration), formalisation for exploration (ns), exploratory x competitiveness (hypothesis failed).
- Substitution innovations: simple designs won (a18).

**Interventions in LLM search that worked or did not.** See section 6.

## 6. Language models

No LLM study tests ambidexterity itself (a29: 22 arXiv hits for "ambidexterity", one on AI in firms; d20: targeted searches for explorer-versus-exploiter agents found nothing). Everything below is an analogue.

**Do LLMs explore on their own? No.**
- GPT-4 basic prompt on a K=5 bandit (gap 0.2), T=500, N=20 replicates: suffix failures in over 60% of runs, like Greedy. Only GPT-4 with chain of thought and an externally summarised history explored well (T=200, N=40), matching Thompson Sampling (b17, c06, c07). A second failure mode is uniform-like spread (b18).
- GPT-3 on the horizon task: short-horizon regret human-like (t(5566)=-0.043, p=.97), but no directed exploration. Whole-task regret M=2.72 vs humans 3.24 (c12).
- Multi-agent peer choice: bimodal lock-in for Qwen2.5-7B, GPT-4, GPT-5 (30 runs, T=50, pA=.6, pB=.5; c01). GPT-5 chose Qwen-7B 294 of 297 times (c04). With homogeneous peers (same LLM) the gain from exploring is small (c05).
- Frontier models on biochemical optimisation: GPT-5.4, Sonnet 4.6, Kimi 2.5, Qwen3.5-397B, GLM-4.7. About 92% of traces state exploration intent, with near-zero correlation to realised novelty. An explicit exploration instruction did not fix it. None beat the mean statistical baseline (d09).
- Telling the model to balance can do worse than random: In-Context Exploration had higher cumulative regret than Random under contextual diversity (c02).

**What changed it, by how much.**
- External computation of the rule: lock-in 50.0% (prompt only) to 23.3% (formula in prompt) to 0.0% (external UCB scores); regret 20.0 to 9.8 (c03). One small model; figure not re-found in the paper text (d16).
- External summary plus chain of thought: from over 60% suffix failures to very few (c06).
- MACE on QA (exploration-phase EM): HotpotQA 0.3133 vs 0.2257 in-context; Math500 L4-5 0.6450 vs 0.6183 vs random 0.5611; GPQA 0.5404 vs 0.4343. Gains persisted when exploration was switched off and transferred to 2WikiMultihopQA with frozen parameters (c02, b22, b23).
- Algorithm-guided support and distillation let smaller models explore better than larger ones (b19, no numbers). RL fine-tuning on reasoning increases exploration and narrows the knowing-doing gap (b20, no numbers). That is training, not a scaffold. HTPO reports +8.6% AIME24 and +6.7% AIME25 over DAPO (d19, snippet).

**LLMs as explore oracle versus exploit oracle.** Reasoning models exploit best; non-reasoning ones need tools or summaries; all lose to linear regression (c08). In-context summaries rescued exploitation at large history: GPT-4, T=4000, reached about 80-90% FracCorrect without CoT. At d=K=5 (GPT-4o, T=1000) k-means summaries were almost as bad as random (c09). As explore oracles LLMs beat random and genre-based baselines on MovieLens (c10). Asking for diverse candidates, or generating one by one, did not change results (c11).

**Evolutionary and search scaffolds (the nearest thing to scaffold ambidexterity).**
- ShinkaEvolve: islands, weighted parent sampling, novelty rejection (similarity above 0.95). Circle packing n=26 sum of radii 2.635983 in under 150 evaluations. Hill climbing plateaus; weighted sampling keeps improving; random is worst. A bandit-chosen LLM ensemble beat a single LLM and a fixed ensemble (c14 to c17).
- LoongFlow: multi-island MAP-Elites plus Boltzmann selection with entropy control. Circle packing target 0.99: 258 evaluations vs OpenEvolve 783; success 3/3 vs 1/3; ShinkaEvolve 0/3. Three runs per arm; vendor preprint (c18, c19).
- QDAIF: poetry QD score 130 (CI 118 to 145) vs random 76 vs fixed seed rewrite 99 (c13).
- AlphaEvolve on 67 math problems rediscovered most best-known results (c21).
- Compute allocation: an interior split beats best-of-N by up to +0.119; adaptive bandit +12.3% over the island baseline; arms 5-20 best (d10, d11). Best search width rises with budget in equation discovery (d13: Bio width 1 to 32 runtime 7,622 s to 1,586 s, train Acc0.1 0.965 to 0.984; three seeds).
- Diversity controls: small, within one SD (d12). Heuresis (3,222 runs, 3 domains): six strategies steer quality and novelty but do not expand the quality-novelty frontier; no idea rated Original; 40 fabrications confirmed in 1,628 scored runs (d17).

**Structure of agent organisation.**
- Equal-token single vs multi-agent (FRAMES, 4-hop MuSiQue; 4 models): above 500 thinking tokens the single agent was best on average at every budget (at 1k: single .418, debate .388, sequential .379, ensemble .333; at 5k: .427 vs .420, .417, .411). At 100 tokens, splits beat single (.370 and .364 vs .290) (d14).
- Pre-registered agent-serving test, about 7,950 conversations: centralised multi-agent lost 28 points (CI -44 to -12) at 2.8 to 6.1 times the tokens (d15).
- Strategy agent plus generation agent in Bayesian optimisation: more stable than one LLM doing both, but one model (llama-3.3-70b), 30 evaluations, no numbers in text (d08).
- MacNet topologies: mesh beat tree beat chain on average; chain suited software, tree suited creative writing; random topologies took about 51.92% less time than mesh; ablating agent profiles cut score 3.67%; majority voting added 0.9% (c20).

**Not LLM evidence.** Surveys of firms using AI (N=245, AI-enabled exploration to innovation beta .604; sensing to AIE beta .530; a27, c22; Haider et al. b=.242, a28) describe human firms. HARMONY (design science, four interviews, c24) and the AMCIS snippet (c25) are opinion-level. Do not cite them as agent evidence (K16).

## 9. What does not transfer, and why

- **Organisational resources.** Human findings about resource level, slack and firm size (Cao, Wenke) depend on money and people. Token budgets are a loose analogue only (d10 budgets are call counts).
- **Culture and trust.** Stretch, discipline, support and trust (a01) assume people with motives. A model has no career risk, no trust, no morale. Only the formal side (target, check, safe log) transfers.
- **Structural separation.** Humans separate units to avoid culture clash, conflicting incentives and measurement conflict. A single LLM has none of these; separating it adds handoff cost (d14, d15).
- **Same-source survey effects.** Many human effect sizes are inflated by common raters (a05, K10). Import directions, not effect sizes, and test them.
- **Long time scales.** Human ambidexterity plays out over years and strategic shifts (sequential switching). Our episodes last minutes. Costs and payoffs per item differ.
- **Market, environment, competition variables.** Dynamism and competitiveness (a15) have only loose agent analogues (task novelty, a competing-answers check).
- **AI-in-firms surveys.** About human firms; zero agent evidence (K16).
- **Homogeneous sub-agents.** Peers that are copies of one model give little to explore among (c05, G4). Human units differ; model copies do not.

## 10. Open questions

1. Pooled effect sizes (k, N, r) for Junni 2013, Mathias 2014, Fourne 2019 and Wenke 2021 were never read. Are balance and product measures reliably different?
2. Does Fourne's structural advantage hold for performance, or only for balance? What do size, dynamism and resources do?
3. Do the He and Wong coefficients replicate outside manufacturing? Only indirect hints (d02, b04).
4. Does a forced explore:exploit scaffold beat explore-only and exploit-only at equal tokens on reasoning benchmarks? No study (d20).
5. Is there any direct explorer-versus-exploiter agent comparison? Not found; closest are d08, d14, d15.
6. Does MACE-style external UCB replicate on non-toy tasks and independent model families? The 50.0% to 0.0% figure was not re-found (d16).
7. How much of the Gibson-Birkinshaw gain survives objective performance data? Not re-tested.
8. Does token budget act like resource level? Partly; budgets in the evidence are call counts (d10, d13).
9. Which diversity control matters most (init, island selection, novelty rejection, island count)? Island count was never ablated (d12, d18).
10. Does the explore-share optimum move with task novelty for LLMs, as it does with R&D intensity for firms?
11. Why does a single agent beat splits above 500 tokens but lose below (d14)? Handoff cost, or context loss?
