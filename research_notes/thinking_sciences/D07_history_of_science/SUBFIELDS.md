# History of science: subfields

History of science studies how scientific knowledge was actually made. For us the useful part is the record of discoveries: what the scientist tried, what failed, and what changed the course. It is divided by method and scale: biographical and notebook studies of individual discoverers, experimental practice, conceptual change, institutions and instruments, and the integrated history and philosophy of science that uses cases to test theories of method. Its main caution is that case studies are few, selected after the success, and often rest on subjective sources such as diaries and memoirs (Klahr and Simon, 2001).

## Dig into next

**1. Cognitive-historical case studies.** Questions: (a) For three to five well-documented discoveries, which operator (analogy, specialise, change representation) came before the breakthrough? (b) Were they applied at an impasse or at the start? Evidence: notebooks, with counts across many cases, not three anecdotes. This tests our placement of operators at S6.

**2. Notebook studies of working scientists.** Questions: (a) How long were dead ends before a switch? (b) What triggered abandoning a line? Evidence: Holmes-style reconstructions of research sequences. These could set the budget fractions for the impasse detector (currently a design choice).

**3. Computational reconstruction.** Questions: (a) Can a loop of generate, test, revise rediscover a historical law from the original data? (b) Which steps are essential in ablation? Evidence: BACON-style program reports.

## Purely conceptual

Debates in historiography (internalism versus externalism, Whig history, relativism) and social-history themes give no procedure. Case histories also suffer survivor bias: failed attempts are rarely recorded, so they are weak evidence for what works, and any step taken from them enters the schema only as [S].
