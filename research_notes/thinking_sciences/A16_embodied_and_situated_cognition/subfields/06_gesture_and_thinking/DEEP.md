## 1. What it is

People move their hands when they think, not only when they talk to others. Gesture researchers ask a causal question: does gesturing help the gesturer think, learn, remember or solve problems? Pouw et al. 2014 say yes, in the sense that gestures are external tools. They give the thinking system a stable physical and visual form, so some work moves from the head to the hands. Wheeler 2013 asks how far that goes. He separates three claims: gestures only help neural thinking (conservative embedded), gestures are part of thinking but inside the skin (radical embedded), gestures and outside things are part of thinking (extended). He finds that gesture data support only the conservative claim (a01, b19, b20).

The empirical core is five kinds of study:
- Dual-task load studies. Gesture while explaining, remember a list at the same time (a02, a06, d03, b10).
- Spatial problem solving with gesture allowed, prohibited or encouraged (a07, a08, d05-d09).
- Learning studies with gesture training (a03, b14, b15, a15).
- Speech-production studies: does gesture help word finding? (a13, b08).
- A meta-analysis of gesture benefit on comprehension (b01-b07).

For our agent this is background only. No LLM study tests the gesture-as-thinking claim itself (K13, c24). The nearest machine analogue is externalising spatial state: write a grid, sketch, or visualise each step. That literature is mixed, and none of it is token-matched (K17, K24).

## 2. History and theories in depth

Four accounts of why people gesture (a11, a12, a13):
1. Gesture as simulated action (GSA, Hostetter and Alibali 2008). Imagery and speech trigger motor and perceptual simulation. Activation spreads to premotor areas. A gesture appears when activation passes a threshold. Three factors set it: simulation strength, threshold height (social setting, effort of inhibiting), and neural coupling. Character-viewpoint gestures come from motor imagery. Observer-viewpoint gestures come from visual imagery. Metaphoric gestures come from spatial image schemas. Cited data: more gestures for dot patterns that must be parsed (2007); more gestures describing a pattern one made than one only viewed (2010); more than twice as many gestures describing a painting from memory as with it present (Wesp 2001) (a12, a14, a27). GSA says gestures are outputs, a by-product and not a tool.
2. Lexical gesture process (Krauss 2000). Gesture feedback primes word retrieval. Evidence was mixed: Frick-Horbury and Guttentag 1998 found more tip-of-the-tongue failures when gesture was restricted; Beattie and Coughlan 1998 did not (a13). The large Kisa 2021 replication found no effect (b08).
3. Information packaging (Kita 2000). Gesture helps chunk spatial imagery into units that speech can carry.
4. Image maintenance. Gesture keeps a visual image active.

Pouw et al. 2014 (a05) argue these accounts are disembodied because they treat gesture as an output of internal states. Their proposal rests on a cost principle (Kirsh 2010, as quoted): processes run wherever it is cheaper to run them. If gesturing is cheap relative to holding a state in mind, work moves to the hand. Parallel findings (a09): Tetris experts rotate pieces physically; people look back at a model just in time rather than memorise; in a VCR task people relied on memory when lookup cost a mouse click and on the screen when it was cheaper. Pouw offers predictions: slow a mouse rotation tool and see if gesture rises; add wrist weights and see if gesture falls (a25). No data were reported. Pouw concedes that the evidence on when and how the system trades inner for outer computation is not conclusive (a29).

Clark (2008) adds "cognitive self-stimulation": gestures are self-generated input that loops back into neural processing, like exhaust driving a turbocharger. Wheeler tests this against first-wave extended cognition (parity principle) and second-wave (complementarity). He prefers the first wave and finds gesture arguments alone insufficient for extension (a04, a23). He also lists three deflationary readings the data cannot exclude: offloading without cognitive status (his phone-number example), gesture as a window on a neural strategy, and tool-like props. Clark's own concession: all benefits might come from a covert, fully neural route (b20).

Timeline of the empirical line:
- 1996: Rauscher, Krauss and Chen prevent gesture and report more disfluency. Kisa 2021 re-examined it: the key interaction was never tested and stats were liberal (b09).
- 1999-2005: Schwartz and Black; Martin and Schwartz. Rotating empty glasses with eyes closed predicts spilling better than imagining; children do better on fractions with movable tiles (a28).
- 2001: Goldin-Meadow et al. dual-task study (d03, d04).
- 2007: Broaders et al. Children told to gesture reveal unspoken strategies and learn more from a lesson (a03).
- 2008-2011: Chu and Kita on mental rotation (d05-d07).
- 2010-2012: Cook et al. on memory and meaningful gesture (b10-b12).
- 2011: Alibali et al. gear problems (d08, d09).
- 2013: Wheeler chapter. 2014: Pouw et al. review.
- 2017: Kirk and Lewis, children's creativity (d11). 2018: Wassenburg et al. fail to reproduce an interference effect (d10).
- 2019: Dargue et al. meta-analysis (b01).
- 2020: Eielts, Pouw et al. Tower of Hanoi (d01, d02); Aldugom et al. (a15).
- 2021: Kisa et al. failed replication of the lexical claim (b08).
- 2024: Frith and Miller (d12); Kersey et al. (b14).

## 4. Mechanisms and models

Explained as functions an engineer could build.

Cost-shifting model (Pouw 2014, Kirsh). Each step of a task can be done two ways: internally (hold and transform a state mentally) or externally (act on a stand-in). Choose external when cost_ext < cost_int. Internal cost rises with load and falls with practice. Gesture rate then rises with difficulty (a16, a17, d05) and falls with practice (d07, a24). Chu and Kita see a path: as-if manipulation, then a flat-hand stand-in, then no gesture. Abacus experts gesture less as they improve (b17). Implementation sketch: a per-step controller with signals (load estimate, past failure on this class, state size) that triggers an external write, with the trigger decaying as the class pass rate rises. No gesture study measures the controller itself; this is Pouw's proposal plus Kirsh's principle.

Load offloading (dual-task logic). Total demand = main task + side task. Gesturing lowers main-task demand, so side-task recall improves. It works only when load is high (long lists significant, short lists not, d03) and only for meaningful movement (b10). The test pattern is portable: run the main task beside a fixed side load and read the side score.

Strategy shift. Prevention does not drop accuracy in the gear task. It moves people from a depictive simulation strategy to an abstract rule (d08). Gesturing makes simulation more likely; not gesturing makes abstraction more likely. Preschoolers who gestured more later found the parity rule (a08). So an external aid changes the route, and route can matter more than accuracy.

Internalisation. Encouraged gesturing in mental rotation improves later gesture-free spatial problems, and gesture rate falls (d06, d07). Reading: computation moves inward. Competing reading: encouragement made people try harder. The within-person comparison is marginal (p .07).

Capacity moderation. Benefit tracks low visual working memory (d02). Spatial span (Corsi) did not interact. Low-capacity people are hurt more by prohibition (a10). The top few percent of visual capacity were slowed by gesturing.

Coupling of simulation and gesture (Marghetis and Bergen, a14). Simulation drives gesture, and gesture shapes simulation. Gesture helps mental rotation only if produced, not merely seen (Goldin-Meadow 2012, cited in a14). This matches production d .91 vs observation d .54 (b02).

Motor context (b13). An unrelated motor task at encoding or at retrieval removes the memory benefit of seeing gesture; matching motor context at both stages restores it. This is a boundary on motor accounts.

Machine analogue mechanism (not gesture). External state is typed into context, then read back. Variants: VoT (prompt the model to visualise the state after each reasoning step, c20), typed grid construction before reasoning (StepGame pruned grid, d13), drawing tools in a loop (Sketchpad, c23), generated images as thoughts (MVoT, c22). Key engineering facts: models read grids well but draw them badly (d15); externalised state is only as good as its construction accuracy (c21, d15); a relation list without geometry did not help (d13).

## 5. What holds up

Holds up:
- Meaningful gesture lightens load in explanation, under taxing loads (d03, b10). The second study (b10) controls for meaningless movement and word finding.
- Gesture helps learning and retention in children for maths (b14, b15, a03). Gesture training held at 4 weeks while action training did not.
- Production beats observation (b02). The pooled comprehension benefit d .61 appears in two meta-analyses (b07). Heterogeneity is high (I2 69%) and the outcome is comprehension of verbal information, not reasoning.
- Gesture rate rises with task difficulty (a16, a17, d05) and falls with practice (d07, b17).

Fails or contested:
- Lexical retrieval. Kisa 2021 (7,969 phrases) found no disfluency cost from preventing gesture. Contested; it concerns speech only (K07).
- Gesture prevention lowers accuracy on spatial problems. Alibali 2011 found equal accuracy. Chu and Kita found allowed equals prohibited. Wassenburg 2018 failed to reproduce the interference effect. No preregistered replication found (K05, K26). The earlier file's stronger wording was corrected.
- Creativity. Adults show no mean gain (b21, d12). The children's fluency gain does not carry to originality or flexibility (d11). Habitual gesturers score higher on all creativity outcomes (d12), which suggests a trait, not an effect.
- Philosophy. The data fit conservative embedded cognition and cannot separate covert neural from gesture-realised cognition (K10).

Moderators found: load (long vs short lists, d03); visual working memory (d02, a15); prior knowledge (children with few strategies do better with action before gesture, b15); meaning of the movement (b10); outcome measure (b05); gesture type (b03, weak); practice (gesture fades, d07).

Weaknesses of the base:
- Founding studies (Goldin-Meadow 2001, Chu and Kita 2011, Alibali 2011) are small; group means are only in figures (d03, d06). The inhibition control rests on 9 children and 10 adults (d04). The Chu and Kita within-person effect is marginal (d07). Dargue subgroups have 2 samples (metaphoric, beat, adolescent).
- Prevention designs confound gesture with the effort of inhibiting. Hostetter and Alibali note that imposed and self-inhibition both give the effect, which helps but does not remove the confound (a06).
- The Eielts 2020 groups were defined per task, so the spontaneous group is self-selected (d01). Counts are 73 (3-disc) and 72 (4-disc); the mismatch is resolved. The study is Eielts, Pouw et al., not Pouw first author (K27).

Interventions that worked: instructing gesture in mental rotation (d06, d07), in creative children (fluency only, d11), in maths learning (b14), and in Tower of Hanoi for low visual capacity (d02). Did not work: encouraging iconic gestures for adult divergent thinking (b21, d12); gesture for word finding (b08); prohibition in gear problems for accuracy (d08).

## 6. Language models

There is no LLM study of the gesture-for-thinking claim. Twelve gesture papers were read; all treat gesture as communication, generation, recognition or safety (K13, c24). Two more searches for finger counting and action-simulation tests found only CFG-Bench (d21), which tests video action description, and sensory norm studies.

Gesture understanding by models (background):
- EcoG-Bench, 811 clips: human 96.9% strict accuracy, Gemini-3-Pro 17.0%, Gemini-3-Flash 7.0%, Qwen3-VL-30B 11.4%, GPT-5-mini 3.3%. With timestamped frames and verified transcript: Gemini-3-Pro 17.0% to 42.9% (+25.9 points); Gemini-3-Flash 7.0% to 48.1% (+41.1) (b23, b24). Interface and observability limit models more than reasoning does (K14).
- MIME, 86 mimed actions: best 52.3% multiple choice, 19.8% free form; under adversarial perturbation all below 10%; chain of thought did not consistently help; in 80% of Gemini 1.5 Flash chain-of-thought failures the gesture was described wrongly (a22).
- GestureGPT zero-shot top-1 44.79% (smart home) and 37.50% (streaming) (c10); gaze context helped more than history; all context together can hurt top-5 (c11). Gestura: exocentric closed-set 84.73%, egocentric open-set 21.71% (b25).
- Culture: recall 63 to 99% but specificity 1 to 61%; DALLE-3 rejects only 10.7% of offensive gesture requests (c08, c09).
- Generation: GPT-4 with a defined intent set picks more appropriate gestures than bare prompts (c03, a20); 43 of 49 real speaker gestures fall in the GPT-4-identified rheme (c04); on an Obama speech 43.2% similar, 43.2% different but appropriate, 13.5% inappropriate (c07). Latency 6.51 to 9.29 s for full output (c05). Small models can distil intent chains (SARGes Qwen1.5-1.8B 50.2%, c17).
- Interaction: GesPrompt N=12, 43.65 s vs 91.11 s gesture-only vs 108.58 s voice-only; SUS 86 vs 74 vs 59 (a18, c13). GIRAF over 70% higher success than language-only in a user study (c12). None of this is about model reasoning.

The nearest analogue: externalising spatial state.
- VoT (GPT-4, c20): route planning success 14.72 (VoT) vs 9.48 (CoT) vs 10.28 (no viz); next-step prediction 55.28 vs 48.61; visual tiling 63.94 vs 54.15; natural-language navigation 59.00 vs 54.00 CoT vs 35.50 no viz. Text-only VoT beat GPT-4V given the image with CoT (c27). Failures: visualisation compliance about 51-52%; state accuracy 26.48 (navigation) and 24.25 (tiling); decisions correct 65 to 77% when the state was accurate; ring navigation CoT 52.5% vs VoT 49.5% (N=200, task reduces to arithmetic) (c21, d17). Weaker models gained unreliably (d17).
- MVoT (Chameleon-7B, c22): over 90% on Maze and MiniBehavior, +7% over Direct on FrozenLake, versus a Direct baseline of about 70%; quality depends on image fidelity (c26). Fine-tuned; figures not re-read in the critique.
- Sketchpad (GPT-4o, c23): +12.7% math, +8.6% vision; V*Bench 80.3%, BLINK spatial 83.9%, correspondence 80.8%.
- StepGame pruned-grid route (d13): LLaMA3.1-70B 44.7 to 86.7 (+42.0); Qwen3-8B 59.2 to 84.8 (+25.6); Qwen3-14B 67.8 to 84.2 (+16.4); Qwen3-32B 67.0 to 84.1 (+17.1); GPT-5.1 74.3 to 88.1 (+13.8); LLaMA3.1-8B 19.8 to 33.3 (+13.5). Relation triples without geometry fell below text for three models (LLaMA-70B 42.1, Qwen3-32B 60.6, GPT-5.1 64.5). SpaRTUN gains up to 8%, ReSQ up to 5%.
- Cost: the grid pipeline used 10x to 63x more tokens (StepGame: Qwen3-32B 2,012 vs 19,218; LLaMA-70B 272 vs 15,472; GPT-5.1 249 vs 15,727. SpaRTUN GPT-5.1 1,145 vs 26,951) (d14). The gains are not at equal budget.
- Adaptive switching (d14): SpaRTUN tokens 6.4% to 12.0% below grid-only with accuracy equal to +0.9; StepGame tokens -0.4% to +10.6% with +0.4 to +1.6 accuracy. SwimBird learned text, vision or interleaved mode choice and beat fixed patterns (WeMath 49.5 vs 38.8, DynaMath 67.2 vs 65.3, V* Bench 85.5 vs 83.8) (d19). It is trained, not prompted.
- Failures: self-drawn ASCII before answering fell below text-only for all base models; generated ASCII was correct only 12 to 50% of the time; GPT-OSS-20B fails to build a correct layout in 56% of cases (d15). Training on construction raised later text-only scores (Qwen3-30B StepGame +9.9, bAbI19 +17.0, SpartQA +43.3; comprehension-only training -6.1 on StepGame); this is fine-tuning, not a prompt (K25). MentisOculi: no gain from self-generated imagery over text on stratified puzzles; GPT-5.1 used about 13x more reasoning tokens under high reasoning with accuracy largely unchanged; unified models could not use even oracle images in some tasks (d18). SVoT: removing images from the trained model cut Sokoban from 78.3 to 35.0 in distribution (d16). STAR: cascading errors on long mazes, success 29.27% vs 25.00% for DeepSeek-V3 (d23). Optical Reasoning: 28.57% fewer reasoning tokens on language tasks by the authors' own metric, a compression result, abstract only (d22).
- Creativity: PainterBench (14 models, 2,700 drawings): agent drawings rated more creative (0.56 vs 0.41) and less recognisable (0.45 vs 0.53) than human drawings; no draw vs no-draw arm (d20).

Reading across: externalising state helps where state is hard to track in text and where the model builds it accurately. It does not help where the task reduces to symbolic computation, where construction is poor, or where horizons are long. The gain is not larger for weaker models (K22). No study matches tokens (K17, K24).

## 9. What does not transfer, and why

- The hand. Part of gesture's benefit comes from the motor system and from meaningful movement (b10, b13). A text agent has no body. Typing a grid is a different act. We borrow the logic (offload state), not the mechanism.
- Working-memory capacity differences. Humans have a small visual store (benefit only for roughly the bottom quarter to third, d02). An LLM has a long context window and no visual store. The Pouw moderator may not map.
- Cost shifting with practice. Human gestures fade as skill is internalised (d07). An LLM does not practise inside one run; the analogue needs memory across runs (S9). Not tested.
- Gesture as communication (speech, pointing, interface). EcoG, MIME, GesPrompt and GestureGPT concern perception and interfaces, not reasoning (K14-K20).
- Word finding. It failed replication (b08). Do not import it.
- Children's maths learning. Gesture training results (b14, b15) concern instruction of children, not in-context learning by an agent.
- Creativity. No adult gain (b21, d12). G8 already says the model does not judge novelty.
- Philosophy of extended mind. Scaffold design needs no commitment (K10).
- Generated images. MentisOculi found no benefit of self-generated imagery over text (d18). Our agent is text only; typed grids are the relevant case.

## 10. Open questions

1. Does typing out a grid or table beat silent reasoning at equal tokens? No study matches tokens (gap 1 open).
2. Is the benefit larger for weaker models? So far mostly no (K22), but grid construction quality confounds it.
3. Are there preregistered replications of gesture prevention on spatial problem solving? None found (gap 5 open).
4. Raw group means for Goldin-Meadow 2001 and Chu and Kita 2011 exist only in figures.
5. Which task features predict that externalised state hurts? Found so far: reduces to arithmetic, low construction accuracy, long horizons, counting-heavy tasks, models that cannot use oracle images (d15-d18, d23).
6. Can an agent learn when to externalise from practice (cost-shifting)? SwimBird learns mode choice by training (d19); no prompt-only or cross-run test exists.
7. Finger counting and action-simulation tests for LLMs: none exist (gap 8 open).
8. Is there any generation task where externalisation helps? None found (gap 9).
9. Is the load effect (long vs short lists) replicated outside the founding lab? Cook 2012 supports gesture benefit but did not repeat the load manipulation.
10. Does the Eielts 2020 capacity effect hold in a larger preregistered sample? Only the bottom quarter of the sample sits in the benefit zone.
