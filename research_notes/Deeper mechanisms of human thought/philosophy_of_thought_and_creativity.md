Scope note: these notes cover the philosophical literature on the structure of thinking (format, composition, concepts), imagination, creativity, inference/reasoning, 4E and extended cognition, and the logic of discovery/abduction, with emphasis on 2020–2026 debates. Neuroscience of creativity networks, mind-wandering, insight neuroscience and semantic-memory search are covered in the earlier report (C:/Users/User/Desktop/AGI/reports/How the brain generates new ideas.md) and are not re-researched here. ## Key Question 1: Language of Thought after Quilty-Dunn, Porot & Mandelbaum (2023) — compositionality, how thoughts combine, how new thoughts form

### Takeaway
As of 2023–2026 the Language of Thought Hypothesis (LoTH) has been re-launched as an empirically-driven "working hypothesis" defined by six clustered properties rather than by Fodor's metaphysics; the authors concede format pluralism (icons, associations, DNN-like structures coexist) and the live dispute is whether the behavioural "footprints" of those six properties require a classical symbolic format or can be produced by non-classical/neural implementations. On novelty, the concrete mechanism LoTH offers is recursive composition of a primitive vocabulary (now formalised as probabilistic program induction), while the formation of genuinely new primitives remains the unsolved "Fodor's puzzle".

### Cited Findings
- Fodor's core package: the Representational Theory of Thought (thinking is a causal sequence of tokenings of mental representations), compositionality (complex representations built from constituents, with constituency a part/whole relation, Fodor 2008), and logical structure (Mentalese contains analogues of connectives and quantifiers). Maps and images "don't seem to support logical operations". — [SEP, Language of Thought (rev. 16 Oct 2023)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/language-thought/)
- Fodor's three main arguments: productivity (finite primitives + recursion explains unboundedly many thinkable thoughts), systematicity of thought (whoever can think John loves Mary can think Mary loves John, Fodor 1987), systematicity of thinking (one mechanical operation such as conjunction elimination applies uniformly to any premises with the right structure, Fodor & Pylyshyn 1988). A standing worry is that infinitary productivity is an idealisation never manifested in performance. — [SEP LoT](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/language-thought/)
- Quilty-Dunn, Porot & Mandelbaum, "The best game in town: The reemergence of the language-of-thought hypothesis across the cognitive sciences", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 46, e261 (2023), argues that after decades of scrutiny "mental representations remain the central posits of psychology" and that the LoT is the format that "routinely crops up". — [Cambridge Core target article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/best-game-in-town-the-reemergence-of-the-languageofthought-hypothesis-across-the-cognitive-sciences/76F46784C6C07FF52FF45B934D6D3542)
- Their six core properties of LoTs: (i) discrete constituents; (ii) role-filler independence; (iii) predicate–argument structure; (iv) logical operators; (v) inferential promiscuity; (vi) abstract content. "These properties cluster together throughout cognitive science." Evidence domains: Bayesian computational modelling, compositional features of object perception, complex infant and animal reasoning, and automatic, intuitive adult cognition. They "grant that the mind may harbor many formats and architectures, including iconic and associative structures as well as deep-neural-network-like architectures." — [QPM 2023 PDF (Philosopher's Annual)](https://philosophersannual.org/42articles/quiltydunnetal-thebest.pdf)
- Definitions in the article (from the full text): Discrete constituents: unlike icons, which "holistically encode features and individuals", LoT representations "comprise distinct constituents corresponding to individuals and their separable features" (in "That is a pink square object", "square" can be deleted without deleting other constituents). Role-filler independence: syntax "maintains independence between syntactic roles and the constituents that fill them" (the role agent is present in both "John loves Mary" and "Mary loves John"; "John" can be agent or patient) — this "captures the rule-based syntactic characteristics of LoT-like compositionality". Predicate–argument structure: "a predicate is applied to an argument to yield a truth-evaluable structure"; images and maps "are assessable for accuracy, but often (perhaps always) fail to exhibit truth-evaluable predicate-argument structure" (citing Rescorla 2009, Kulvicki 2015, Camp 2018). Logical operators: NOT, AND, OR, IF as discrete constituents that compose into larger structures. Inferential promiscuity: representations "should not only encode information, they should be usable for inference in a way that is automatic and independent of natural language". Abstract content: LoT symbols are not limited to sensory properties, in opposition to concept empiricism (Barsalou 1999, Prinz 2002). — [QPM 2023 PDF](https://philosophersannual.org/42articles/quiltydunnetal-thebest.pdf)
- The concrete composition mechanism they endorse is the "probabilistic language of thought" (PLoT) of Goodman, Tenenbaum & Gerstenberg (2015): "a set of primitives with basic operations for their combination (such as the lambda calculus, e.g., Church ...)"; "Primitives correspond to atomic concepts, which are recursively combined to form concepts of arbitrary complexity"; "All one must do is define a set of primitives, and a set of rules for combination and the system is capable of constructing a potentially infinite string of well-formed formulae"; Bayesianism adds a noisy likelihood and a simplicity prior. — [QPM 2023 PDF](https://philosophersannual.org/42articles/quiltydunnetal-thebest.pdf)
- Their conclusion is explicitly modest and pluralist: "many representational formats across many cognitive systems are LoTs"; "We believe in other vehicles of thought, including associations ..., icons ..., and much more"; "We don't deny the successes of DCNNs"; "It remains open that DNNs might mimic the performance of biological perception and cognition ... by implementing core features of LoTs". — [QPM 2023 PDF](https://philosophersannual.org/42articles/quiltydunnetal-thebest.pdf)
- Comparative/developmental evidence they lean on: the four-cup disjunctive-syllogism task (Mody & Carey 2016) in children, chimpanzees pulling both strings when boxes are opaque (Engelmann et al. 2021, read as simultaneous representation of two possibilities), baboon success on the four-cup task (Ferrigno et al. 2021), and a grey parrot (Pepperberg et al. 2019). — [QPM 2023 PDF](https://philosophersannual.org/42articles/quiltydunnetal-thebest.pdf)
- The article drew 30 commentaries plus an author response. Titles include "Stop me if you've heard this one before: The Chomskyan hammer and the Skinnerian nail", "A language of episodic thought?", "Properties of LoTs: The footprints or the bear itself?" (McGrath, Russin, Pavlick & Feiman), "Neither neural networks nor the language-of-thought alone make a complete game" (Oved, Krishnaswamy, Pustejovsky & Hartshorne), "Evidence for LoTH: Slim pickings" (Pereplyotchik), "Concept learning in a probabilistic language-of-thought" (Colombo), "The computational and the representational language-of-thought hypotheses" (Chalmers), "Linguistic structure and the languages-of-thought" (Dupre). — [Cambridge Core commentary listing](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/volume/901042062D203290E441000007F45322?pageNum=15); [author response page](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/languageofthought-hypothesis-as-a-working-hypothesis-in-cognitive-science/77F01F8388EDE0B266E718AD463A575B)
- Lupyan's commentary ("Is language-of-thought the best game in the town we live?") grants some behaviours can be interpreted as LoT-compatible but argues LoT remains "a poor way to understand human cognition". — [Lupyan commentary (NSF PAR)](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10478984)
- Attah & Machery argue that QPM's commitment to representational pluralism undermines their case, since "the evidence they present is consistent with the operation of the other representational formats that they are willing to accept". — [Attah & Machery commentary](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/ATTIEO)
- Dupre's commentary treats the QPM methodology (inferring format from behavioural capacities) as potentially linking linguistic theory to comparative psychology. — [Dupre, BBS](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/abs/linguistic-structure-and-the-languagesofthought/0B184F4F41E3A375F64BC904CD779C19)
- The authors' response ("The language-of-thought hypothesis as a working hypothesis in cognitive science", BBS 46, e292) says the target article attempted to link "broad swaths of evidence" via "Abstract, symbolic, compositional codes, that is, language-of-thoughts (LoTs)", that commentators "raised concerns about the evidence" and "offered fascinating extensions", and that the reply aims to "highlight the many specific empirical questions to be answered in the next decade and beyond". (Full reply text was not accessible.) — [Cambridge Core response](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/languageofthought-hypothesis-as-a-working-hypothesis-in-cognitive-science/77F01F8388EDE0B266E718AD463A575B)
- Precursor: Mandelbaum, Dunham, Feiman, Firestone, Green, Harris, Kibbe, Kurdi, Mylopoulos, Shepherd, Wellwood, Porot & Quilty-Dunn, "Problems and Mysteries of the Many Languages of Thought", *Cognitive Science* (2022), argues evidence supports LoT but reveals variation in LoTs across cognitive systems, developmental stages and species, and lists open questions about the rules/representations of different LoT-based systems. — [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10428979)
- Newest hardware-level critique: Piccinini, "Neural Hardware for the Language of Thought: New Rules for an Old Game" (arXiv, 11 Oct 2025) argues whether cognition uses a LoT depends on how neural hardware works; "Classical LOT (Fodor 1975) is ruled out"; the best-supported version is a Nonclassical LoT in which some neural representations mirror some structure of natural language but encode information nondigitally and are processed by nondigital neural computations that use syntactic structure alongside many other features. — [arXiv 2510.10251](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10251)
- Classical connectionism debate recap: Fodor & Pylyshyn (1988) argue eliminative connectionism cannot explain the nomic necessity of systematicity; five response families (deny representations; accept implementation role, Marcus 2001; deny systematicity/productivity, Rumelhart & McClelland 1986, Dennett 1991; alternative explanations; deny classical structure, Chalmers 1990/1993, Smolensky 1991, van Gelder 1991); Fodor & McLaughlin (1990) claim distributed representations lack concatenative structure, Aydede (1995, 1997) replies that non-concatenative structure can be broadly classical. — [SEP LoT](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/language-thought/)
- How new concepts form under LoTH: if children learn concepts by hypothesis formation and testing (HF), a regress follows because the hypothesis already uses the concept; Fodor's answer is radical concept nativism ("innate" = unlearned; Fodor 2008: HF requires representing hypotheses like "The C things are the ones that are green or triangular", which presupposes the concepts). Responses: Margolis (1998) non-HF acquisition; Carey (2009) bootstrapping, which Fodor (2010) and Rey (2014) call circular; Shea (2016) argues connectionist models can learn non-HF. The SEP concludes the regress is a general obligation to explain concept acquisition, not a LoTH-specific problem. — [SEP LoT](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/language-thought/)
- Rule & Piantadosi, "The end of radical concept nativism" (arXiv, May/July 2025) identifies three points where Fodor's view departs from human cognition (expressive power, conceptual structure, concept possession), formalises them with computer science and information theory, and concludes "there is an important sense in which people do indeed learn new concepts". — [arXiv 2505.18277](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18277)
- Independent empirical pressure on the "thought = language" reading: Fedorenko, Piantadosi & Gibson, "Language is primarily a tool for communication rather than thought", *Nature* 630: 575–586 (June 2024) reviews a double dissociation between the language network and thought (language can be impaired with reasoning intact and vice versa) and argues language "is not a prerequisite for complex thought, including symbolic thought" but "a powerful tool for the transmission of cultural knowledge"; Fedorenko: the linguistic mechanisms "are not really engaged when we think". — [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2024/what-is-language-for-0703); [paper PDF (gwern mirror)](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2024-fedorenko.pdf)

### Inferences
- Implementable claim: the live, operationalised version of LoTH is the PLoT — a small primitive vocabulary, typed combination rules (lambda calculus), a noisy likelihood and a simplicity (description-length) prior. On this picture a "new thought" is a new well-formed formula; a "new concept" is a program found by search over the space of combinations. This is the most mechanistic account of composition in the philosophical literature and is directly re-implementable (Church-style program induction).
- The six properties function as a behavioural test battery: a system (human or artificial) exhibits LoT-like format to the extent it shows role-filler independence (systematic recombination), truth-evaluable predication, explicit negation/disjunction, and automatic inference that is independent of natural language. The McGrath/Pavlick "footprints or the bear" and Piccinini objections jointly imply that passing the battery does not settle whether the implementation is classical/digital — this is exactly the question that LLM-era cognitive science is now fighting over.
- The Fedorenko result is compatible with LoTH (LoT is not natural language) but is damaging for "thinking = inner speech" views (see Q5), and it pushes the LoT community toward Mandelbaum et al.'s "many LoTs" (modality-specific, developmentally staged symbol systems).
- The weak point for a theory of novelty is the primitive base: composition explains productivity but not the acquisition of new primitives. Carey's bootstrapping (Q2) and Rule & Piantadosi's information-theoretic argument are the two current routes around Fodor's puzzle.

### Gaps
- The full text of the QPM author response (e292) was behind a paywall; only its abstract and commentary titles were accessible, so the detailed replies to Lupyan, Pereplyotchik, McGrath et al. and Attah & Machery are not reported.
- Colin Klein's 2025 "What do Language Models Model?" (argument that human linguistic capacity relies on supralinear formats while transformers support only linear formats) surfaced in a search summary without a retrievable URL; not verified.
- No peer-reviewed 2024–2026 reply by Quilty-Dunn specifically to the deep-learning critiques was found.

## Key Question 2: Concepts and conceptual change — how new concepts are formed and old ones combined; what is conceptual engineering

### Takeaway
Philosophers now treat "concept" as a contested natural kind: Machery argues the word picks out no single kind (prototypes, exemplars, theories do different jobs), Margolis & Laurence defend pluralism with atomic cores, Carey argues new primitive concepts arise through Quinian bootstrapping (placeholder symbols + analogical mapping), and Camp argues thought runs in several non-sentential formats (maps, "characterisations", "frames") that structure what is salient. Conceptual engineering is the explicitly normative programme of evaluating and revising concepts/meanings, now split between semanticist (Cappelen) and psychologist (Koch, Isaac, Nefdt) accounts of what is being engineered.

### Cited Findings
- Theories of concept structure and their standard objections: classical (definitions; fails on typicality effects and failed analyses, e.g. Gettier 1963), prototype (Rosch & Mervis 1975; fails the dog-made-to-look-like-a-raccoon case, Keil 1989, and compositionality, the PET FISH problem, Fodor & Lepore 1996; replies by Prinz 2002, Robbins 2002), conceptual cores (Osherson & Smith 1981), theory theory (Carey 1985, 2009; Gopnik & Meltzoff 1997; holism objection Fodor & Lepore 1992), atomism (Fodor 1998, Millikan 2000; content fixed by causal relations). — [SEP, Concepts (rev. 2019)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/concepts/)
- Pluralism: Laurence & Margolis (1999) propose a single concept with an atomic core linked to prototypes and theories; Weiskopf (2009) proposes multiple co-referring concepts (CAT1, CAT2...); Vicente & Manrique (2016) treat these as polysemous senses. Eliminativism: Machery, *Doing Without Concepts* (2009), argues "concept" does not pick out a natural kind so theorists should speak of prototypes, theories etc.; replies by Hampton, Lalumera, Gonnerman & Weinberg, Margolis & Laurence, Samuels & Ferreira, Weiskopf (all 2010). — [SEP Concepts](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/concepts/)
- Empiricism: Prinz (2002) holds concepts are copies or combinations of perceptual representations; Barsalou (1999) treats concept possession as simulation; objections concern abstract concepts (TRUTH, NINETEEN; Adams & Campbell 1999), logical concepts, and imagery (Machery 2007). — [SEP Concepts](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/concepts/)
- Fodor's puzzle restated: concept learning as hypothesis testing requires already having the target concept, so primitives must be innate (Fodor 1975, 1981); Fodor (2008) later says concepts are "acquired" by biological processes, not learned. Margolis & Laurence reply that many concepts are learned (Margolis 1998; Laurence & Margolis 2002) and that culture supplies concepts like PURGATORY via books and sermons (Margolis & Laurence 2011). — [SEP Concepts](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/concepts/)
- Carey's Quinian bootstrapping (The Origin of Concepts, 2009): children acquire number concepts by building an analogy between sets and the verbal count list; placeholder symbols are mapped onto schematic representations of small sets, and generalising that mapping yields number concepts that go beyond anything innate. — [Rule & Piantadosi 2025 (arXiv) summary of Carey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.18277); Carey's key move is to separate developmental primitives from definitional/computational primitives: new developmental primitives can be learned and bootstrapping does not build them from antecedently available concepts. — [Warwick lecture notes on Carey vs Fodor](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/philosophy/intranets/undergraduate/modules/philosophyofeducation/2015-16/notes_week_7.pdf)
- Objections to bootstrapping: Fodor presses how Carey can separate belief change from conceptual change (if every belief change made a new concept, one could never revise a belief about the same concept); BBS commentators (Rips et al.) ask why bootstrapping does not simply supply a translation between old and new systems, undercutting the claimed incommensurability. — [BBS commentary listing](https://resolve-he.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/abs/beyond-the-building-blocks-model1/6F4E58CCCD69D9B72AD45C58F454DBCB)
- Margolis & Laurence ("Beyond the building blocks model", BBS 2011) accept Carey's rejection of the building-blocks model but argue new primitives can be learned by other, non-bootstrapping mechanisms, that bootstrapping is not a unitary process, and propose their own Syndrome-Based Sustaining Mechanism model; an Edinburgh thesis surveying candidate mechanisms concludes almost every one has some leverage against Fodor but doubts they form one natural kind "concept learning mechanism". — [Edinburgh Research Archive thesis](https://era.ed.ac.uk/items/9a9f3a2e-92f7-4b6a-b2a2-2c4a737bcc8f)
- Camp, "Thinking with Maps" (Philosophical Perspectives 21, 2007): thought need not have language-like form; "if a thinker's representational needs were sufficiently simple, it might think entirely with maps"; differences in combinatorial principles between sentential and cartographic systems "produce substantive differences in how they represent and subserve reasoning" and predict distinct patterns of cognitive ability and breakdown. — [PhilArchive record](https://philarchive.org/rec/CAMTWM)
- Camp, "Why Maps Are Not Propositional" (2018): propositions have a structure that is digital, universal, asymmetrical and recursive, and "there is little positive evidence that maps exhibit these features". — [PhilPapers search listing](https://philpapers.org/s/maps)
- Camp, "Perspectives and Frames in Pursuit of Ultimate Understanding" (in Grimm ed., Varieties of Understanding, OUP 2019, 17–45): framing devices "suggest an intuitive way of thinking about their subjects" and "express open-ended perspectives, which produce structured intuitive characterizations of particular subjects"; frames are double-edged, blinding us to known features that don't fit and to "unknown unknowns"; frames are "tools for thought" whose products can be epistemic achievements in their own right. — [PhilArchive record](https://philarchive.org/rec/CAMPAF-7)
- Camp, "Imaginative Frames for Scientific Inquiry: Metaphors, Telling Facts, and Just-So Stories" (in Levy & Godfrey-Smith eds., The Scientific Imagination, OUP 2019, 304–336) distinguishes metaphor from telling details, just-so stories and analogies and analyses the gaps frames open between representation and reality. Earlier, "Two Varieties of Literary Imagination" (2009): "pretense involves the imaginative manipulation of what we take to be so, while metaphor reconfigures how we think about what is so." — [PhilArchive CAMIFF-2](https://philarchive.org/rec/CAMIFF-2); [PhilArchive CAMTVO](https://philarchive.org/rec/CAMTVO)
- Camp's 2022 Burman Lectures define a perspective as "an open-ended disposition to notice, structure, and respond to information in an intuitive, holistic way". — [Umeå Burman Lectures 2022](https://www.umu.se/en/events/burman-lectures-2022-1_11313412/)
- Conceptual engineering, canonical definition: "the project of investigating, evaluating, and, when necessary, repairing, replacing, or augmenting the elements of our own conceptual toolkit"; Cappelen's *Fixing Language* (2018) proposes an "Austerity Framework" that "makes no reference to concepts" and answers the Strawsonian "change of subject" challenge via coarseness of topics and an externalist metasemantics. Sawyer objects that concepts are needed to account for topic continuity. — [NDPR review of Fixing Language](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/fixing-language-an-essay-on-conceptual-engineering); [Sawyer, CJP](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/canadian-journal-of-philosophy/article/abs/role-of-concepts-in-fixing-language/EEAC6BCE743C2EAB589570F32B465E13)
- Burgess, Cappelen & Plunkett (eds.), *Conceptual Engineering and Conceptual Ethics* (OUP 2020), with an introductory "guided tour" explaining the two labels and the foundational issues; Chalmers (2020, Inquiry) "What is conceptual engineering and what should it be?"; Chalmers, Blackburn, Eklund and Richard variously suggest philosophy might just be conceptual engineering. — [Cappelen & Plunkett intro (PhilArchive)](https://philarchive.org/rec/CAPAGT); [Fischer 2020 on conceptual control](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/75145/1/Fischer_2020_Conceptual_control_final_accept.pdf)
- Isaac, Koch & Nefdt, "Conceptual Engineering: A Road Map to Practice" (Philosophy Compass 2022) maps the logical space of projects by processes, targets/goals and methods; Koch, Löhr & Pinder, "Recent work in the theory of conceptual engineering" (Analysis 2023) frames the central debate as semanticism vs psychologism and argues neither gives the full story; Koch (2020, Synthese) "Engineering what?" asks what concepts are such that they can be engineered; Koch (2024, Mind & Language) gives a psycholinguistic pro tanto reason to retain the original word during revision; Koch (2024, APQ) addresses the implementation problem. — [Koch PhilArchive listing](https://philarchive.org/s/Steffen%20Koch); [Koch 2020](https://hss-opus.ub.rub.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/year/2022/docId/9487)
- Belleri (2021) distinguishes "semantically conservative" inquiries (no semantic change permitted) from "semantically progressive" ones to answer the discontinuity-of-subject-matter objection. — [Belleri 2021 PDF](https://cful.letras.ulisboa.pt/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Belleri-D-2021.pdf)
- Boden's account of combinational creativity (new combinations of familiar ideas, e.g. the heart as pump, the atom as solar system) is the standard philosophical name for conceptual combination as a creative mechanism, and she notes creative combinations require the new association to have some point, not mere juxtaposition. — [Boden 1998 text](https://cse.msu.edu/~cse841/papers/Boden.html)

### Inferences
- Implementable claim (Carey): a new primitive can be introduced as an uninterpreted placeholder symbol embedded in a structured external system (a count list, a notation), then given content by analogical mapping onto existing representations and by inductive generalisation of the mapping. This is a general recipe for concept creation: adopt a syntax first, fill in semantics by analogy.
- Implementable claim (Camp): a "frame" or "characterisation" is not a proposition but a weighting structure over features (prominence, centrality) that governs what gets noticed and how new information is assimilated; changing the frame (metaphor, analogy) reconfigures the whole structure at once. This is a candidate mechanism for Boden's transformational creativity at the level of concepts rather than rule systems.
- Conceptual engineering supplies the normative loop: evaluate a concept by its function (what inquiry needs), then redesign. The semanticist/psychologist split matters for an implementation: psychologism (Koch, Isaac) targets the actual mental representation and its inferential role; semanticism (Cappelen) targets word meaning fixed by external facts and concedes the engineer has little control.
- The PET FISH compositionality problem is the standard reason prototype-based concept combination cannot be the whole story; any combinational-creativity model must handle emergent features (the properties of the combination not inherited from either component).

### Gaps
- Camp's 2015 "Logical Concepts and Associative Characterizations" and her detailed structural account of characterisations (prominence/centrality dimensions) could not be accessed; the claims above are from abstracts.
- The empirical conceptual-combination literature (Hampton's attribute-inheritance model, Wisniewski's relation-linking vs property-mapping, emergent features) was not retrieved; the SEP treats combination only via the pet-fish objection.
- Nefdt (2024), "Concepts and Conceptual Engineering", was listed but not read.

## Key Question 3: Imagination — structure, constraints, epistemic value, imaginative resistance, role in generating possibilities

### Takeaway
The 2016–2026 literature has converged on a "puzzle of imaginative use" (Kind & Kung): the same faculty seems both free (transcendent) and world-tracking (instructive), and the dominant solution is constraints — Kind's reality and change constraints, Williamson/Langland-Hassan structure-based constraints — with 2023–2024 objections (Kinberg & Levy, Gauker, Stuart) that content constraints are either too deterministic, too permissive, or exclude knowledge gained by breaking constraints. Structurally, the field is split between sui generis attitude accounts (Walton, Nichols & Stich single-code), reductive accounts (Langland-Hassan 2020: imagining is belief/desire/judgment about the possible), skill accounts (Kind 2020) and eliminativism (Nanay 2023), and most now agree creative imagination is a use of ordinary imagining rather than a separate type.

### Cited Findings
- Taxonomy (SEP, rev. 12 Jan 2026): attitude vs imagistic imagining (Van Leeuwen 2013, 2014); propositional, objectual and "imagining X-ing" contents (Walton 1990); inside vs outside perspective (Williams 1973, Ninan 2016); "constructive imagining" as the process of generating a representation (Van Leeuwen); spontaneous vs deliberate (Walton 1990); transcendent vs instructive uses (Kind & Kung 2016). — [SEP, Imagination](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/imagination/)
- Walton, *Mimesis as Make-Believe* (1990): artworks are props that generate prescriptions to imagine; Walton (2015) later held such prescriptions are necessary but not sufficient for fictionality. Nichols & Stich (2000, 2003) single-code hypothesis: belief and attitude imagining share a representational format, yielding belief-like inferential relations (tested by Leslie 1994 on children's tea-party pretence). Currie & Ravenscroft, *Recreative Minds* (2002): imagination simulates other mental states off-line. — [SEP Imagination](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/imagination/)
- Langland-Hassan, *Explaining Imagination* (OUP 2020, open access): attitude imagining is "epistemically safe" cognition about the possible or fantastical and reduces to other attitudes (e.g. believing something merely possible); hypothetical reasoning, pretence, fiction and creativity are each explained by judgments, beliefs, desires and decisions, with no sui generis state; the book also considers how deep learning might help explain the creative process. Chasid (2025) objects that attitudinal imagining is irreducible. — [PhilPapers LANEI-2](https://philpapers.org/rec/LANEI-2); [SEP Imagination](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/imagination/)
- Other structural positions: Kind (2020) imagery-based imagining is a skill (can be done more or less well, is under intentional control, improves with practice); Wiltsher (2023) imagination as a process governed by a "lens"; Nanay (2023) eliminativism ("imagination" is not a unified kind; use "mental imagery"); imagistic imaginings are hybrids of image plus propositional label (Fodor 1975, Peacocke 1985, Noordhof 2002); Van Leeuwen (2013) argues some contents cannot be imaged; Balcerak Jackson (2016) argues an image-based account is needed to separate imagining from supposing and conceiving. — [SEP Imagination](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/imagination/)
- Constraints: Kind & Kung (2016) "puzzle of imaginative use"; Kind (2016, 2018) reality constraint (imaginings capture the world as it is) and change constraint (imagined changes follow logically from the change); structure-based constraints built into psychological architecture (Langland-Hassan 2016; Williamson 2007, 2016; Miyazono & Tooming 2024) vs content-based constraints imposed by beliefs and experience (Kind; Myers 2021). — [SEP Imagination](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/imagination/); [Kind, "Imagining Under Constraints" (Claremont)](https://scholarship.claremont.edu/cmc_fac_pub/532)
- Objections to constraint accounts: Levy & Kinberg argue that if constraints are applied explicitly the epistemic role of imagination "collapses into trivial hypothetical reasoning", while implicit constraints leave the mechanism unexplained; Kinberg & Levy (2023) add that intuitive physics is biased so cannot guarantee reliable imaginings; Gauker (2024) poses a dilemma (content constraints are either deterministic, failing on non-closed systems, or too permissive); Stuart (2020) says constraint accounts leave no room for knowledge gained by breaking constraints. — [Levy & Kinberg, Synthese 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-021-03156-x); [SEP Imagination](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/imagination/)
- Langland-Hassan's sceptical slogan: imagination cannot improve one's epistemic position any more than "handing yourself a dollar" improves one's finances (2016: 62); his "On Choosing What to Imagine" explains how imaginings that guide action and inference can also be subject to the will. — [Levy & Kinberg 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-021-03156-x); [Kind & Kung volume review, NDPR](https://ndpr.nd.edu/news/knowledge-through-imagination/)
- Epistemic value: modal pessimists (Van Inwagen 1998) vs optimists (Chalmers 2002, Yablo 1993, Williamson 2007/2016, Ichikawa 2016); imagistic optimists/propositional pessimists (Gregory, Kung 2010); Balcerak Jackson (2018) recreative imagination gives indirect insight into possibility; Lam (2018, 2021) imagistic and non-imagistic imagination on a par. Scientific knowledge: Galileo's thought experiment; Gendler (2004) and Nersessian (1992, 2007) optimists, Norton (2004) and Weisberg (2013) pessimists. Empirical knowledge: Spaulding (2016) imagination cannot justify but "generates ideas that other processes evaluate"; Miyazono & Tooming (2024) constrained imagining can generate new justification; Myers (2021) it preserves justification; Badura (2021) it can justify. — [SEP Imagination](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/imagination/); [Badura & Kind eds., Epistemic Uses of Imagination (2021, OAPEN)](https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/57844?show=full)
- Imaginative resistance: Gendler (2000) — attempting to imagine morally repugnant propositions generates resistance, but supposing them does not; Weatherson (2004) — suppositions are "coarser" than imaginings, which carry more fine-grained detail. — [SEP Imagination](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/imagination/)
- Imagination and creativity: Polanyi (1966) imagination narrows the solution space; Carruthers (2002) pretend play is practice for creative thinking; Gaut (2003) and Stokes (2014) imagination's freedom from truth and detachment from action suit it to creativity; Kind (2022) imagination combines ideas in novel ways; Currie & Ravenscroft distinguish recreative from creative imagination; Arcangeli (2022) and Kind & Langkau (forthcoming) hold creative imagination is a use of other imagination types, not a separate type; Langkau (2025) creative imagination "intentionally manipulates imagined contents, guided by subjective value"; Hills & Bird (forthcoming) argue imagination is not necessary for creativity. — [SEP Imagination](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/imagination/)
- Kind, *Imagination and Creative Thinking* (Cambridge Element, July 2022, series ed. Frankish) asks what imagination and creativity are, whether each requires the other, whether they are skills that can be developed, and whether AI systems could have them; it includes a case study on imagination and creativity in machines. — [Cambridge Core Element page](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/abs/imagination-and-creative-thinking/5DF5018E25423007505F1BF0C899012D)
- Studies cited in the SEP creativity entry: imagined creatures closely resemble familiar ones (Karmiloff-Smith; Ward), showing existing concepts constrain creative imagination; Finke's image-transformation work links imagery manipulation and vividness to creative problem solving. — [SEP, Creativity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creativity/)
- Recent 2023–2025 extensions: Michaelian (2024) episodic memory as imagining the past; Van Leeuwen (2023, 2025) religious belief as make-believe; Levy (2024) and Munro (2024) conspiracy theories as make-believe/fantasy; Sullivan-Bissett (2024) implicit biases as imaginings; Blomkvist (2022) and Blomkvist & Marks (2023) imagination as skill and aphantasia. — [SEP Imagination](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/imagination/)

### Inferences
- Implementable claim (Kind's constraints): an instructive imagining is a simulation initialised from a world model (reality constraint) with one or more stipulated changes, then rolled forward only by consequences that follow from the change (change constraint). This is exactly a counterfactual simulation with controlled interventions; the Levy/Kinberg objection is that if the rollout is just explicit inference, imagination adds nothing beyond hypothetical reasoning, so the distinctive contribution must lie in implicit, imagistic or embodied rollout dynamics.
- The field's consensus that creative imagination is "ordinary imagining used for a purpose" (Arcangeli, Kind & Langkau, Langkau 2025) implies creativity is not a separate faculty but a control policy over imagining: deliberate manipulation of imagined contents guided by a value signal. Hills & Bird's dissent (imagination not necessary) and Langland-Hassan's reduction both push the mechanism down into belief/desire/decision machinery plus generative sampling.
- Ward/Karmiloff-Smith's "structured imagination" result (imagined novel creatures retain bilateral symmetry, sense organs, etc.) is the empirical counterpart of the reality constraint and sets a baseline: default imaginings are near-neighbours in concept space; transformation requires explicit constraint-dropping (Boden) or frame-shift (Camp).
- Nichols & Stich's single-code hypothesis is the architectural reason imagination can be inferentially productive: pretend inputs run through the same inference engine as beliefs, so the system can derive non-obvious consequences of a supposition.

### Gaps
- Williamson's "Knowing by Imagining" (2016, in Kind & Kung) and Van Leeuwen's "Imagination is where the action is" (2011) were not accessed directly; their positions are reported via the SEP.
- Langland-Hassan's specific proposal about deep learning (GAN-style generation plus evaluation) in the creativity chapter of Explaining Imagination was mentioned by New Books Network but its details were not retrievable.
- No source quantified "imaginative resistance" or its recent (2020s) treatments beyond Gendler/Weatherson.

## Key Question 4: Philosophy of creativity — Boden's three types and conceptual spaces; creativity as virtue/skill; rationality, explicability, determinism; the "paradox of creativity"

### Takeaway
Boden's framework (combinational / exploratory / transformational creativity operating on generative "conceptual spaces", with named transformation heuristics) remains the only account in philosophy that is directly computational; the live debates since 2018 concern the definition (Hills & Bird drop value and replace it with a four-component disposition: fertility, originality, imagination, motivation; Gaut, Paul & Stokes insist on agency), whether creativity is a virtue (Kieran yes via intrinsic motivation; Gaut no), and whether creativity can be causally explained at all (Kronfeldner: yes, determinism permits novelty; creativity excludes only certain causes such as copying or routine; Simonton's blind-variation model vs Gabora's critique).

### Cited Findings
- The SEP entry "Creativity" (first published 16 Feb 2023, by Paul & Stokes) distinguishes person/process/product senses; the psychological "standard definition" is new + valuable; Boden's P-creative (new to the creator) vs H-creative (new to history); four candidate process conditions: surprise (Boden), originality (Kronfeldner 2009, 2018), spontaneity (Kronfeldner; Gaut 2018), agency (Gaut 2018; Carruthers; Kieran; Stokes; Paul & Stokes 2018). — [SEP, Creativity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creativity/); [listing confirming Paul & Stokes authorship](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/18509)
- Value condition contested: Stokes (2008, 2011) argues defining creativity by value is uninformative "as with defining a carburetor by its usefulness"; the immoral-creativity counterexample (De Quincey 1827; Novitz 1999 denies, Livingston 2018 criticises); Livingston (2018) proposes instrumental effectiveness, Gaut (2018) "good of its kind", Hills & Bird (2018) reject both. — [SEP Creativity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creativity/)
- Hills & Bird, "Creativity Without Value" (in Gaut & Kieran eds., Creativity and Philosophy, Routledge 2018): "it is possible to be creative but disposed only to produce objects of no value"; their replacement definition: creativity is "the disposition or set of linked dispositions of an individual: to have many ideas (fertility); which are novel (originality) and generated through use of the imagination (imagination); and to carry through these ideas to completion (motivation)". Arguments: creativity can have wholly negative outcomes (creative torturer); we recognise creativity without assessing value (Stephenson's Britannia Bridge: "we need to know how imaginative, novel and fertile were his ideas, and how strong was his motivation"); and it is "not psychologically realistic" to posit two dispositions in Herschel or Tesla, one producing valuable and one worthless ideas — "both are explained by the use of his imagination". They also dismantle the Kantian "original nonsense" argument: it shows originality is insufficient, not that value is necessary; and if nonsense has no content it cannot be a product of imagination, "which is essentially representational", so imagination rather than value distinguishes nonsense from creativity. — [Hills & Bird PDF (Bristol)](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/ws/files/118143340/Creativity_Without_Value.pdf)
- Hills & Bird, "Against Creativity" (PPR 2018/2019) extends this: creativity is a disposition toward originality, imagination, fertility and motivation rather than value; critics reply that removing value obscures the normative role creative products play and that "creative" is a thick epistemic concept. — [Academia.edu record](https://www.academia.edu/96265310/Against_Creativity); [critical paper, PhilPapers SNCNW](https://philpapers.org/rec/SNCNW)
- Boden's own definition and types (1998 text): "At the heart of creativity lie constraints"; a genuinely creative idea is one which cannot be produced by the same generative rules as familiar ideas; conceptual spaces are "established styles of thinking" (chess, sonnets, tonal harmony) each defined by "the particular set of generative principles" with "its own dimensions, pathways, and boundaries"; combination-theorists define creativity as "novel combinations of old ideas", which Boden calls "not wholly irrelevant" but unable to explain the most intriguing cases; exploration = navigating the space (Bach's Forty-Eight); transformation = changing the space (Schoenberg dropping the home-key constraint). — [Boden 1998, "Creativity and Artificial Intelligence" (text)](https://cse.msu.edu/~cse841/papers/Boden.html)
- Boden's transformation heuristics: "dropping a constraint" (non-Euclidean geometry, atonal music), "consider the negative" (Kekulé moving from strings to rings), and "vary the variable" (treat a fixed value as variable). Heuristics also steer exploration ("Protect your queen" directs chess search). Applied "at a relatively deep level of the generative system", consider-the-negative "can transform the space so fundamentally that very different sorts of location are created" (Kepler's ellipses). — [Boden 1998 text](https://cse.msu.edu/~cse841/papers/Boden.html); [Wiggins on Boden](https://axon.cs.byu.edu/~dan/673/papers/wiggins.pdf)
- Boden's three kinds of surprise: unfamiliarity (against statistics), unexpectedness (doesn't fit an existing style), impossibility ("cannot have entered anyone's head"); combinational creativity is surprising only statistically; transformational creativity yields the deepest surprise. — [Griffith repository paper on Boden](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/a6270ecf-57e3-4a96-9e6d-f1cf5c527a2f/content)
- Boden on computational models: AARON (Cohen) explores its drawing space but one-armed acrobats are "unimaginable by AARON" until a constraint is dropped; EMI (Cope) composes in composers' styles via "signatures"; Lenat's AM generates mathematical concepts and follows hunches though "the precise extent of AM's creativity is unclear"; EURISKO's heuristics change heuristics so it can "explore and transform" its own style; Sims's genetic-algorithm images use mutation and crossover with human selection. She also flags that "AI will have less difficulty in modelling the generation of new ideas than in automating their evaluation". — [Boden 1998 text](https://cse.msu.edu/~cse841/papers/Boden.html); [Griffith repository](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/a6270ecf-57e3-4a96-9e6d-f1cf5c527a2f/content)
- Standard critique: Boden's "exact definition of conceptual spaces remains elusive" and "it is not clear from Boden's writings ... how she would define the constraints which give rise to a particular conceptual space"; Wiggins formalised the framework as a creative-systems framework. — [Wiggins PDF](https://axon.cs.byu.edu/~dan/673/papers/wiggins.pdf); [Griffith repository](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/a6270ecf-57e3-4a96-9e6d-f1cf5c527a2f/content)
- Nanay (2014) modifies Boden: what matters is the creator's experience of the process as having these features (an experiential account). — [SEP Creativity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creativity/)
- Agency: Gaut (2018) argues creative things must come from agents (snowflakes, sunsets are not creative); the snowboarder whose tracks form a pleasing pattern is not creative; Gaut's constitutive account (2014) defines creativity as a disposition involving ability and motivation exercised through "choice, evaluation, understanding, and judgment"; Paul & Stokes: if you learn a stone arrangement was made by wind and waves you should withdraw the judgment of creativity though you may still find it beautiful. Arnheim (2001) and Boden (2018) attribute creativity to natural processes, challenging the agency requirement. — [SEP Creativity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creativity/)
- Stokes (2014): creative thought requires "cognitive manipulation" that is non-truthbound; imagination suits this because it is free of the requirement to represent accurately. Stokes is also read as holding creativity is a form of praise tied to intentionality, so accidental production does not count. — [SEP Creativity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creativity/); [Human Creativity and AI (arXiv 2025)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.08001)
- Virtue/skill: Kieran (2014, 2018) argues "exemplary creativity" is a virtue because it requires intrinsic motivation; Gaut (2014, 2018) objects that intrinsic motivation is not conceptually necessary (Eisenberger & Rhodes 2001; Watson and Crick, Picasso vs Matisse, McCready), so creativity is not an Aristotelian virtue; Zagzebski (1997) treats creativity as an epistemic virtue because truth-conducive; Gaut (2014) argues creativity can be taught, against Young (1759) and Kant (1790), distinguishing algorithms (which preclude creativity) from heuristics (which do not); Hájek applies this to philosophical heuristics. — [SEP Creativity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creativity/)
- Explicability and determinism: Plato's divine inspiration; Kant and Schopenhauer's innate genius and "free play"; Hospers (1985) complexity and generalisability worries; Hausman (1975/1984) argues creative products are "genuinely novel" in a metaphysical sense so cannot be causally explained; Kronfeldner (2009) replies that determinism permits novelty and creativity excludes only certain kinds of causes (copying, routine). — [SEP Creativity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creativity/)
- Kronfeldner, "Creativity Naturalized" (2009): creativity is compatible with determinism and naturalistic explanation; distinguishes anthropological, historical, psychological and metaphysical concepts of creativity. Kronfeldner, "Explaining Creativity" (2018, Routledge Handbook): the Darwinian approach "fails to give an explanation of creativity, because it confuses conceptual issues with explanation". — [Kronfeldner 2009](https://pub.uni-bielefeld.de/record/1590727); [Kronfeldner 2018, PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/rec/KROEC)
- Blind variation and selective retention: Campbell (1960) is the key model; Simonton argues an idea's creativity is inversely related to its "sightedness" and treats blindness as both chance-like sub-awareness generation and the creator's uncertainty about whether an idea advances the work; Simonton has conceded "expertise, remote associates, and strategy play substantial though subsidiary roles". Gabora replies that this concession is inconsistent with Darwinism and that variation-and-selection was introduced to solve a paradox about biological inheritance that "does not exist with respect to creative thought". — [Simonton summary](https://scottbarrykaufman.com/study-alert-creative-thought-as-blind-variation-and-selective-retention-why-creativity-is-inversely-related-to-sightedness/); [Gabora critique](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.2210)
- Fedyk & Xu (2020) define creative ideas as "potentially valuable improbable constructions", an attempt to formalise the originality/value tradeoff. — [PhilSci-Archive 18509](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/18509)
- Shevlin (2019) argues comparative psychology shows creative intelligence in non-humans and proposes a notion of "creative intelligence" independent of human-centric value. — [Shevlin PDF](https://henryshevlin.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/Creativity-December-2019.pdf)
- Evaluation and productivity (SEP creativity §5.4): people good at evaluating ideas also tend to be good at generating them (Runco et al. 1990–2004); the "productivity theory" holds good ideas come from generating many and discarding most (Sawyer 2012; Simonton 1988); Pauling: "If you want to have good ideas you must have many ideas" and the skill is knowing which to discard. Externalisation (§5.5): creators continually build on hunches via sketches and outlines (John-Steiner 1985, 70+ creators; Perkins 1981 on Picasso's 61 Guernica sketches); sketching helped people discover and solve more problems (Verstijnen 1997); externalisation is key for complex tasks because working memory is limited (Finke et al. 1992). — [SEP Creativity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creativity/)
- AI and creativity (SEP §6): weak artificial creativity is granted (AARON, EMI, The Painting Fool, DALL·E); strong requires process conditions (surprise, originality, spontaneity, agency); Lovelace (1843) denied the Analytical Engine could originate anything; Halina (2021) argues AlphaGo's move 37 shows insight and satisfies Lovelace's originality worry; the entry concludes agency "might be the final frontier" for strong artificial creativity (Paul & Stokes 2021). — [SEP Creativity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creativity/)
- Gaut, "Creativity and Rationality", JAAC 70(3): 259–270 (2012) exists and defends the rationality of creative processes; Gaut's 2010 Philosophy Compass survey covers the definition of creativity, its relation to imagination, and "whether the creative process is rational". — [JAAC issue page](https://academic.oup.com/jaac/issue/70/3); [St Andrews portal](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/creativity-and-imagination/)

### Inferences
- Implementable claim (Boden): a conceptual space is a generative grammar plus constraints; exploratory creativity is search within it guided by heuristics; transformational creativity is a meta-operation on the grammar itself with a small, enumerable repertoire of operators (drop a constraint, negate a constraint, promote a constant to a variable). Transformations are most powerful when applied at a "deep" level of the generative system. The standing problem she herself identifies is evaluation, not generation.
- Implementable claim (Hills & Bird): creativity decomposes into four measurable dispositions — fertility (idea count), originality (novelty), imagination (generation through representational manipulation rather than copying/rule-following), and motivation (carry-through). Value is handled downstream by a separate judgment/taste faculty (their reading of Kant's "taste disciplines genius"). This cleanly maps onto a generate (imagination × fertility) → select (taste) architecture, and is consistent with the SEP finding that generation and evaluation skill correlate.
- The "paradox of creativity" as it appears in this literature is really two puzzles: (a) explicability — how can something be both genuinely novel and causally explained (Hausman vs Kronfeldner: resolved by locating novelty in the absence of copying/routine causes, not in acausality); (b) rationality — how can a rational process yield what could not have been derived (Gaut: via heuristics rather than algorithms; Boden: via meta-level transformation). Both resolutions locate creativity in process type, not in indeterminism.
- Agency debates (Gaut, Paul & Stokes) imply any system claiming creativity must own the choice/evaluation steps, which is why philosophers treat the evaluation side, not sampling, as the hard part for machines — matching Boden's remark.

- No source explicitly uses the phrase "paradox of creativity" as a technical term; the content is reconstructed from the explicability and rationality debates.

## Key Question 5: Reasoning and inference — Mercier & Sperber; Boghossian and Broome on inference; dual-process in philosophy (Frankish, De Neys); thinking as inner speech and whether thought requires language

### Takeaway
Philosophical accounts of inference split between agency views (Boghossian's Taking Condition: inferring requires taking the premises to support the conclusion; Broome: reasoning is a conscious, rule-governed operation on attitude contents) and their critics (Wright, McHugh & Way, Hlobil), while Mercier & Sperber's interactionist theory relocates "reason" as one more intuitive metarepresentational module whose function is producing and evaluating reasons in social exchange — explaining myside bias and laziness as features, and predicting solitary reasoning rarely corrects intuitions. Dual-process theory survives but in a reformed shape (De Neys 2025: deliberation is multifunctional — control, generation, justification, regulation), and the inner-speech literature plus Fedorenko et al. (2024) now largely reject the claim that thought requires language while retaining a role for inner speech as a cognitive tool.

### Cited Findings
- Boghossian (2014, "What is inference?", Philosophical Studies): inference is subject to the Taking Condition — "it necessarily involves the thinker taking his premises to support his conclusion, and drawing the conclusion because of that fact" — which vindicates inference as an exercise of agency; taking need not be an occurrent meta-belief; following a rule such as Modus Ponens "explains and rationalizes your accepting q on the basis of your accepting p and p → q". — [Boghossian 2014, Phil Studies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-013-0104-z)
- Critics: Wright argues the Taking Condition should be dropped; McHugh & Way ("Against the Taking Condition") reject it; Hlobil accepts a constraint on theories of inference but proposes instead an inferential analogue of Moore's Paradox as the adequacy test and argues Boghossian's, Broome's and Wright's accounts all fail it. — [Wright 2012](https://crispinjwright.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/wright-2012-boghossian.pdf); [McHugh & Way](https://philarchive.org/rec/MCHATT); [Hlobil](https://philarchive.org/rec/HLOABW)
- Broome, *Rationality Through Reasoning* (2013): reasoning is "a rule-governed operation on the contents of your conscious attitudes" — applying a rule to attitude-content pairs (from belief in p and belief in if p then q, derive belief in q) — and an act you perform intentionally or knowingly; Pettit objects that Broome's rule-following discussion does not answer the Wittgenstein/Kripke problem. — [Pettit, "Broome on reasoning and rule-following"](https://swh.princeton.edu/~ppettit/papers/2016/Broome%20on%20Reasoning%20and%20Rule-following%20preprint.pdf); [Southampton symposium paper](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/366994/1/Broome_20on_20Reasoning_20teorema_20web.pdf)
- Mercier & Sperber vs the Taking Condition: they use "inference" far more broadly than philosophers; what they call "reasoning" is partly constituted by a representation of premises as good reasons for the conclusion, but that representation "is usually a result (often a kind of rationalization) of the move from the premises to the conclusion and not a cause of that move". — [Phil Studies 2018 paper on inference and M&S](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11098-018-1142-3)
- Mercier & Sperber, "Bounded rationality in a social world" (2020, Routledge Handbook of Bounded Rationality): "We suggest that reason is another of these metarepresentational mechanisms. It takes several representations as inputs—the premises and conclusion of an argument—and delivers as output an intuitive metarepresentational judgment of the quality of the support relation between premises and conclusion." Reason "evolved because it allowed individuals to find justifications and arguments, and to evaluate the justifications and arguments offered by others"; its function is "to address problems of coordination and communication by producing and evaluating reasons used as justifications or as arguments in communicative interactions". Reasons in everyday argument "are generated and evaluated quickly and effortlessly, and, most of the time, with no insight as to why such and such reason was chosen". — [Mercier & Sperber 2020 PDF](https://www.dan.sperber.fr/wp-content/uploads/2020_mercier_sperber_bounded-rationality-in-a-social-world.pdf); [abstract page](https://www.dan.sperber.fr/?p=1731)
- Their indictment of dual-process System 2: "System 2 suffers from a massive myside bias"; "System 2 is lazy when it comes to evaluating our own reasons"; "Confirmation bias and laziness were supposed to be System 1, not System 2 features, but the evidence says otherwise"; "By and large, solitary reasoning doesn't correct mistaken intuitions" (bat-and-ball example). They note that dual-process theories, while a "very successful export", are "in retreat within the psychology of reasoning itself", and criticise the "bundle of contrasting features" (slow/fast, automatic/controlled, explicit/implicit...) used in lieu of a principled distinction. — [Mercier & Sperber 2020 PDF](https://www.dan.sperber.fr/wp-content/uploads/2020_mercier_sperber_bounded-rationality-in-a-social-world.pdf); [Sperber site](https://www.dan.sperber.fr/?p=1731)
- Mercier concedes the argumentative theory is "partly anchored in dual process accounts" (not a wholesale rejection); a reviewer notes the theory's stated limit: it "does not work when we reason alone or with people who share our beliefs". — [Informal Logic reply](https://doaj.org/article/7d6ee10a9a814469bf31d273c6580e9b); [Phil Studies 2018 paper](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11098-018-1142-3)
- Frankish, "Dual-process and dual-system theories of reasoning" (Philosophy Compass 2010): type 1 processes are fast, automatic, non-conscious; type 2 slow, controlled, conscious; biases are typically attributed to type 1 and logical responses to type 2; dual-system theories go further and posit two reasoning systems ("the two minds hypothesis"). Frankish's *Mind and Supermind* argued a dual-systems perspective is implicit in everyday belief-desire psychology; his "Systems and levels" (in Evans & Frankish eds., In Two Minds, 2009) maps the two systems onto the personal/subpersonal distinction. — [Frankish 2010 (ORO)](https://oro.open.ac.uk/22114); [Frankish, Systems and levels](https://oro.open.ac.uk/22100)
- De Neys, "Defining deliberation for dual-process models of reasoning" (Nature Reviews Psychology, 2025): deliberation ("System 2") "has received limited attention" in its conceptualisation; it "should be understood as multifunctional", with four functions — "response control, response generation, response justification, and regulation" — and current debates concern whether the systems run in parallel or serially and how individuals switch between intuitive and deliberate thinking (Ackerman & Thompson 2017; De Neys 2023; Evans 2019; Pennycook et al. 2015; Stanovich 2018). (Read from a local preprint copy; public URL not retrieved.)
- Inner speech as expression vs representation: Langland-Hassan argues inner speech episodes lack the contents needed to match the meaning of thoughts; inner speech represents word sounds and, derivatively, words and sentences, so "we think about sentences rather than with them". — [OSU talk abstract](https://philosophy.osu.edu/events/professor-peter-langland-hassan-contents-inner-speech); Langland-Hassan & Vicente (eds.), *Inner Speech: New Voices* (OUP 2018) presents inner speech as "a language-like phenomenon that has been seen as a form of thought or intrinsically related to it", a topic peripheral in analytic philosophy until the 1990s. — [NDPR review](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/inner-speech-new-voices/)
- Fernyhough's Vygotskian "Dialogic Thinking" model: private speech develops from social dialogue and is internalised as verbal thought; adult inner speech retains dialogic properties; inner speech "requires language but functions outside linguistics"; Fernyhough holds intelligence does not require language (infants, animals); Fernyhough & Borghi (2023, Trends in Cognitive Sciences) frame inner speech as both "language process and cognitive tool". — [Nature review of The Voices Within](https://www.nature.com/articles/532032a); [Hearing the Voice](https://hearingthevoice.org/2013/06/07/life-in-the-chatter-box/); [Publishers Weekly](https://www.publishersweekly.com/9780465096800)
- Carruthers ("The cognitive functions of language", BBS 2002) rejects both the claim that language is conceptually necessary for thought and that it is the medium of all conceptual thinking, proposing instead that natural language is "the medium for non-domain-specific thinking, serving to integrate the outputs of a variety of domain-specific conceptual faculties". Critics: the vehicle of thought must be explicit and natural language is not (Aguilera); language is a nonrepresentational system of conventional signals, so words are a tool we think with rather than a medium we think in. — [Carruthers BBS 2002](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/abs/cognitive-functions-of-language/0CDF19278D6DB1919B2DEE655893AB46); [Aguilera, argument from explicitness](https://api.philpapers.org/rec/AGUTLA)
- Bermúdez, *Thinking without Words* (2003): defends realistically construed non-linguistic thought in animals and infants (Carruthers: this half is "largely successful") while arguing language is needed for second-order/meta-representational thought (the "rewiring" hypothesis), which Martínez-Manrique & Vicente (2010) criticise along with Carruthers's inner-speech theory. — [Carruthers review of Bermúdez](https://faculty.philosophy.umd.edu/pcarruthers/Bermudez.htm); [Martínez-Manrique PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/s/Fernando%20Mart%C3%ADnez-Manrique)
- Vicente & Martínez-Manrique document "unsymbolized thinking": with Hurlburt's descriptive experience sampling, some subjects report thinking that does not involve words or any other symbols. — [PhilArchive VICTLA](https://www.philarchive.org/rec/VICTLA)
- Fedorenko, Piantadosi & Gibson (Nature 2024): language network not engaged during many thought tasks; global aphasics retain reasoning; conclusion that language "only reflects, rather than gives rise to, the signature sophistication of human cognition". — [MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2024/what-is-language-for-0703); [McGovern Institute](https://mcgovern.mit.edu/2024/06/19/what-is-language-for)
- The SEP Concepts entry's summary of the priority debate: Fodor (1975) and Pinker (1994) hold thought precedes language; Carruthers (1996, 2002) that some thinking happens in language; Lupyan (2012) that language augments concepts; linguistic determinism is largely rejected, relativity still debated. — [SEP Concepts](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/concepts/)

### Inferences
- Implementable claim (Mercier & Sperber): reason is a reason-evaluator — a function from (premises, conclusion) pairs to an intuitive quality-of-support score — whose outputs are generated lazily and asymmetrically (strong when evaluating others' arguments, weak when evaluating one's own). The architecture predicts that idea quality improves under adversarial dialogue, so a solitary thinker should simulate interlocutors or externalise critique. Their point that the "taking" is typically post hoc rationalisation inverts Boghossian: the support judgment follows rather than causes the inferential move.
- De Neys's four functions of deliberation include "response generation", which is the first mainstream dual-process framework to assign idea generation (not just checking) to deliberate processing; combined with Mercier & Sperber's view that most inference is intuitive and unconscious, the emerging picture is intuitive generation with deliberate control/justification layered on top, plus unresolved switching rules.
- On language: the philosophical consensus (Carruthers's weakened thesis, Fernyhough's "cognitive tool", Langland-Hassan's "thinking about sentences") converges with Fedorenko's neuroscience on a tool view: language is a format for integrating, rehearsing, serialising and socially transmitting thought, not its medium. For idea generation, inner speech is best modelled as a self-directed communicative act (dialogic) that recruits the reason-evaluator above.

### Gaps
- The full text of Boghossian 2014 and Broome 2013 were not read; positions are from secondary summaries and critical replies.
- Mercier & Sperber's specific quantitative claims about group reasoning (e.g. success rates on Wason tasks in groups) were not retrieved.
- De Neys 2025 has no retrieved URL; the citation rests on a local preprint copy.
- Fernyhough & Borghi 2023 (TiCS) was not read directly.

## Key Question 6: 4E cognition and the extended mind — where thinking happens; how tools, notation and environments generate ideas (Clark & Chalmers, Menary, Gallagher, Malafouris, Kirsh, Heersmink)

### Takeaway
Extended-mind theorists claim external media are constituents of cognition (parity principle), and the strongest versions for idea generation are Menary's cognitive integration/enculturation (new notations acquire non-derived content and re-purpose neural circuits), Kirsh's functional account of why external representations add cognitive power (persistent referents, shareable objects of thought, reformulation), and Malafouris's Material Engagement Theory ("thinging": intentions and affordances co-constitute each other in the act of making). The constitutive claim remains contested (Adams & Aizawa's coupling-constitution fallacy, Rupert's systems view), but the functional claims about externalisation generating ideas are empirically supported in the creativity literature (Perkins, Verstijnen, John-Steiner).

### Cited Findings
- 4E taxonomy (SEP Embodied Cognition, 2021): embedded (environment reduces cognitive load: Hutchins 1996 on ship navigation), extended (environmental resources are constituents of the cognitive system: Clark & Chalmers 1998; Menary 2008), enactive in three strands — autopoietic (Varela, Thompson & Rosch; Di Paolo 2005), sensorimotor (O'Regan & Noë 2001; Noë 2004) and radical (Hutto & Myin 2013; Chemero 2011) — plus embodied as umbrella term. — [SEP, Embodied Cognition](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/embodied-cognition/)
- Clark & Chalmers (1998): Otto's notebook vs Inga's memory; parity principle — if an external process would count as cognitive when done in the head, it counts as cognitive when done outside; Wilson (2004) makes the same case for multiplication on paper. Clark argues paper gives "ways of thinking that might otherwise be unavailable to us" and the brain "directs the construction of cognizing systems" some of which include external constituents. — [SEP Embodied Cognition](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/embodied-cognition/)
- Objections: Adams & Aizawa's coupling-constitution fallacy (causal coupling does not make a process part of cognition; bowling-ball example) and marks of the cognitive (intrinsic non-derived content; a uniform science, brain-tool systems being a "genuine motley"); Clark replies that brains also contain non-intrinsic processes and the Otto+notebook system as a whole can be cognitive; Rupert (2004): tools are used by cognitive systems, not constituents (axe analogy); Shapiro (2019): parity arguments treat bodies as irrelevant, conflicting with embodiment. — [SEP Embodied Cognition](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/embodied-cognition/)
- Hutto & Myin (2013) "basic minds without content" seek to replace representational explanation; the SEP presses the "representation-hungry" problem (thinking about unicorns; Clark & Toribio 1994). — [SEP Embodied Cognition](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/embodied-cognition/)
- Menary's cognitive integration is a vehicle externalism: cognitive processes "are not merely 'scaffolded' by tools and structures in the environment; they are partially constituted by external structures"; developed via enculturation, where new mathematical symbols introduced by expert mathematicians have non-derived content (content not dependent on an interpreting agent or convention), answering Adams & Aizawa's mark-of-the-cognitive objection for mathematics. — [Extended mathematical cognition (PMC 2020)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7437646/); Menary & Gillett (2022) describe cognitive artifacts integrated through enculturation, with older neural circuits redeployed to reading, writing and mathematics. — [Heersmink/OECS entry](https://oecs.mit.edu/pub/bx21plqj)
- Kirsh, "Thinking with external representations" (AI & Society 25(4), 2010): the obvious answer that external representations save memory and computation "is only part of the story"; he lists seven ways they enhance cognitive power, including creating persistent referents, serving as a shareable object of thought, and helping coordinate thought. — [PhilArchive KIRTWE](https://philarchive.org/rec/KIRTWE)
- Heersmink's taxonomy of cognitive artifacts (2013): "human-made, physical objects that functionally contribute to performing a cognitive task"; genera representational (iconic, indexical, symbolic) vs ecological (spatial, structural); his 2016 account of agent–artifact integration uses dimensions of informational malleability, intensity and kind of information flow, trustworthiness, procedural and informational transparency, and individualisation. — [PhilArchive HEEATO](https://philarchive.org/rec/HEEATO); [Heersmink 2016, Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11097-015-9432-0)
- Malafouris, *How Things Shape the Mind: A Theory of Material Engagement* (MIT Press 2013): things are "cognitive extensions of the human body"; "artifacts are not external products of human thinking, but rather integral parts of the thinking process itself"; metaplasticity — "the plastic human mind is embedded and inextricably enfolded within a plastic material culture"; on the potter: "it is as if the potter's intentions inhabit the clay and the affordances of clay bring forth the potter's intentions". — [MIT Press Bookstore](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780262528924); [Templeton feature](https://www.templeton.org/?p=4714); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambros_Malafouris)
- Malafouris, "Thinking as 'thinging': Psychology with things" (Current Directions in Psychological Science 29(1): 3–8, 2020): thinking is usually understood as something we do about things in their absence; "thinging" is "the kind of thinking we do primarily with and through things"; "withness and throughness takes precedence over aboutness"; mind-stuff has no fixed location so pertains equally to brains, bodies and things. — [Berkeley EDRL summary](https://edrl.berkeley.edu/guest-speakers/lambros-malafouris); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lambros_Malafouris); a JMB review notes the book is largely devoted to undermining internalist perspectives in cognitive archaeology. — [JMB review](https://jmb-online.com/pdf/03/JMB-34-3-4-2013-005.pdf)
- Gallagher (How the Body Shapes the Mind, 2005; Action and Interaction, 2020): primary and secondary intersubjectivity — perceptual, pre-theoretical understanding of others and shared attention — are our main mode of social cognition rather than mentalising. — [SEP Embodied Cognition](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/embodied-cognition/)
- Clark, *Surfing Uncertainty* (2016): minds constantly predict their inputs; only prediction errors feed forward; Clark locates "the neural engines of prediction nested within the larger organizational forms of the active body, and enmeshed in the transformative structures of our material, social, and technological worlds" (active structuring of the environment). The boundary debate continues in BBS: "Markov blankets do not demarcate the boundaries of the mind". — [Buskell review of Surfing Uncertainty](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/24299/1/Andy%20Clark%27s%20Surfing%20Uncertainty%20__%20Reviewed%20by%20Andrew%20Buskell.pdf); [BBS Markov blankets](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/markov-blankets-do-not-demarcate-the-boundaries-of-the-mind/06C279F5BFC0C3E3181BD3E9C6485892)
- Empirical support for externalisation as a generative step (from the SEP creativity entry): creators build on hunches through sketches and outlines (John-Steiner 1985; Perkins 1981: Picasso's 61 Guernica sketches, Beethoven's drafts, Darwin's notebooks); sketching helped people discover and solve more problems (Verstijnen 1997); diagrams improved inventive ideas (Mayer 1989); mental visualisation suffices only for simple tasks, externalisation is key for complex ones because working memory is limited (Finke et al. 1992). — [SEP Creativity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/creativity/)

### Inferences
- Implementable claim (Kirsh/Clark): external representations are not storage but transformation devices: they stabilise a referent so it can be re-inspected, re-parsed and reinterpreted (Kirsh's persistent referents), make thought shareable and criticisable (connecting to Mercier & Sperber's argumentative loop), and allow operations (rearranging, sketching, erasing) that the internal medium cannot perform. Idea generation under this view is an iterate–externalise–re-perceive loop.
- Implementable claim (Menary/Malafouris): a new notation or material medium does not merely express pre-formed thoughts; it changes the space of thinkable operations (non-derived content of new mathematical symbols; the clay's affordances bringing forth intentions). This is the 4E version of Carey's placeholder-symbol mechanism and of Boden's constraint-based conceptual spaces: notation is a conceptual space one can enter.
- The constitution debate (Adams & Aizawa, Rupert) does not affect the functional claims; even if the notebook is "merely" causally coupled, the generative benefit of externalisation stands on the empirical evidence above.

### Gaps
- Clark's "Material Symbols" (2006) and "Magic Words" (1998) — his explicit account of language as a cognitive tool that restructures thought — were not retrieved.
- Menary's own writing on writing/notation (Cognitive Integration, 2007; "Writing as thinking", 2007) was not accessed; the account is reported via a 2020 PMC paper developing it.
- Hutchins's "material anchors for conceptual blends" (2005), the most explicit cognitive-science account of how artifacts generate new conceptual structure, was not retrieved.
- Gallagher's Enactivist Interventions (2017) was not accessed; his position is represented only by the intersubjectivity work.

## Key Question 7: Discovery vs justification; abduction (Peirce) and inference to the best explanation as the logic of hypothesis generation; recent work on the "generation problem"

### Takeaway
Philosophers now distinguish Peirce's abduction (explanatory reasoning in generating hypotheses) from modern IBE (explanatory reasoning in justifying them, Harman 1965, Lipton 1991/2004), and the Reichenbach/Popper claim that discovery admits no logic has been largely replaced by a multi-level picture: Hanson's retroductive schema, Simon's heuristic search in a problem space (BACON, KEKADA), Magnani's creative vs selective abduction, Nersessian's model-based reasoning (analogy, visual modelling, thought experiment), Darden's schema-instantiation strategies, and Nickles's "generative justification". The generation problem reappears inside IBE itself as van Fraassen's "bad lot" objection — the best of the considered hypotheses may still be bad — which is the formal reason hypothesis generation cannot be ignored.

### Cited Findings
- Two senses of abduction: Peirce's historically first sense concerns explanatory reasoning in generating hypotheses; the modern sense concerns explanatory reasoning in justifying them (IBE). Harman (1965) coined "inference to the best explanation" and treated enumerative induction as a special case; Lipton (1991, 2004) is the main defender, adding in 1993 that the best explanation must also be "good enough" and arguing in 2004 that Bayesians should be explanationists. — [SEP, Abduction (Douven; rev. 18 June 2025)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/abduction/)
- Peirce treated abduction as the only logical operation that introduces a genuinely new idea, "to describe the process of generating hypotheses in order to explain surprising facts"; the predominant scholarly interpretation isolates abduction to the first stage, "the psychological and logical process of generating or suggesting new hypotheses". — [arXiv 2111.08054, Abductive Inference and Peirce 150 years later](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.08054); [arXiv 2604.08016 survey of abduction in LLMs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.08016)
- Peirce himself wavered between abduction as forming an explanatory hypothesis and as choosing one; a PhilArchive paper contrasts a "Generative Interpretation" (instinctive insight-driven generation) with a "Pursuitworthiness Interpretation" (rule-governed ranking of hypotheses) and argues both have serious problems — the generative reading conflicts with Peirce's insistence that abduction is a kind of inference. — [PhilArchive MOHBTI](https://philarchive.org/rec/MOHBTI)
- Niiniluoto's Oxford Handbook of Peirce chapter (2024) reviews post-war readings of abduction as a logic of discovery (Hanson), a logic of pursuit (Laudan) and a logic of justification. — [Oxford Handbook chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/56073/chapter/441680628)
- Objections to IBE: van Fraassen's (1989) argument of the bad lot — IBE may yield "the best of a bad lot" — which the SEP says works against the absolute rule but not comparative versions (Kuipers), and which "is essentially about generation: whether the best explanation is among the hypotheses considered" (responses: Schupbach 2014; Dellsén 2017; Lipton's add-the-negation move judged of little help); van Fraassen's informativeness and Dutch-book arguments (replies by Maher 1992, Skyrms 1993, Douven 1999); rule-circularity of Boyd's no-miracles defence (Laudan, Fine; Psillos 1999: rule-circular but not viciously). — [SEP Abduction](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/abduction/)
- Bayesian reconstructions: Lipton (2004) explanatory considerations guide priors and likelihoods; Weisberg (2009) uses them to weight partitions; Okasha, McGrew, Dellsén treat them as heuristics when priors are unavailable; Psillos (2000): abduction selects hypotheses for testing, Bayesian testing follows; Roche & Sober (2013) argue explanatoriness is evidentially irrelevant, McCain & Poston reply; Douven (2013) and Douven & Wenmackers (2017) show probabilistic IBE can outperform Bayes' rule in some settings; Douven & Schupbach (2015) find people's updates are influenced by explanatory considerations. Recent books: Douven, *The Art of Abduction* (2022); Dellsén, *Abductive Reasoning in Science* (2024). — [SEP Abduction](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/abduction/)
- Discovery/justification: Reichenbach (1938) treated discovery as an empirical, psychological matter; Popper separated conceiving ideas (psychology) from testing (logic). Critiques: Curd's (1980) "discovery machine objection"; Brannigan (1981) and Schaffer (1986) treat "discovery" as a retrospective social label; Laudan (1980) adds a third context of pursuit. — [SEP, Scientific Discovery (rev. Oct 2022)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-discovery/)
- Hanson (1958–1965): hypothesis generation follows an abductive/retroductive logic starting from surprising phenomena and proposing a hypothesis type that would explain them (Kepler on Mars); objections: Lugg (1985) historically inadequate; Harman (1965) too permissive; Koehler (1991), Brem & Rips (2000) question rationality. Magnani (2000, 2009) separates selective abduction (choosing among known hypotheses) from creative abduction (generating new ones); Thagard (2010) and Thagard & Stewart (2011) recast abduction neurally and explain creativity as combinations of representations (critiqued by Pasquale & Poirier 2016). — [SEP Scientific Discovery](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-discovery/)
- Discovery as problem solving: Simon (1973, 1977) treats discovery as heuristic search through a problem space; BACON rediscovered Kepler's third law from data, KEKADA (Kulkarni & Simon 1988) simulated the Krebs-cycle discovery; heuristics include means-ends analysis and decomposition. Objections: Hempel (1985) computers are confined to given concepts; Gillies (1996) programs cannot decide which data are relevant or what form of law to seek; real problem spaces are ill-defined. — [SEP Scientific Discovery](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-discovery/)
- Nersessian (1992, 1999, 2009): conceptual innovation uses three forms of model-based reasoning — analogical modelling, visual modelling, simulative modelling/thought experiments — and conceptual change comes from building new models that abstract and integrate source and target domains; Hesse (1966) positive/negative/neutral analogies; Holyoak & Thagard (1996) multi-constraint analogy; Dunbar (1997, 2001): in-vivo lab studies show analogies for fixing problems come from near domains, analogies for new concepts from distant ones. — [SEP Scientific Discovery](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-discovery/)
- Kuhn: discovery is an extended process beginning with anomaly (violated paradigm expectation); the oxygen case shows no single discoverer or moment. Darden (1991, 2002, 2009; Craver & Darden 2013): reasoning strategies for generating, appraising and revising mechanism hypotheses, with "schema instantiation" as the generation strategy. Nickles (1985, 1989): "generative justification" — a claim is justified by showing it could have been derived from established results ("discoverability"); Schaffner (1993): weak appraisal during formation vs strong testing; Campbell (1960): blind variation and selective retention. — [SEP Scientific Discovery](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-discovery/)
- Whewell (1840): the initial "happy thought" cannot be analysed but colligation and verification can. Jantzen (2016) argues that without a logic of discovery scientific success would be a miracle. Machine discovery 2015–2021: Pietsch on theory-ladenness in data-intensive science; Leonelli (2020); Stokes et al. (2020) antibiotic discovery; Tamaddoni-Nezhad et al. (2021) machines as "co-developers" of knowledge; the entry flags an opacity problem for deep learning. — [SEP Scientific Discovery](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-discovery/)
- Recent work on abduction and creativity: Desclés (2024) argues abduction is essential for forming new knowledge (planetary motion, hieroglyph decipherment); Redmond & López-Orellana (2023) propose treating hypothesis generation as a conditional; a 2025 paper defends Peirce's pragmatism as explaining scientific novelty via "cotary propositions". — [Desclés 2024 (ILLC)](https://resources.illc.uva.nl/CRAIPS/uploaded_files/inlineitem/1cr.aips.2.descles.pdf); [Redmond & López-Orellana, Artefactos](https://revistas.usal.es/cinco/index.php/artefactos/article/view/31543/29972)
- Aizawa & Headley (2022, 2025) extend abduction to composition and "constitutive abduction". — [SEP Abduction](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/abduction/)

### Inferences
- Implementable claim (Hanson/Peirce schema): generation starts from a surprising fact C; the operation is to retrieve or construct a hypothesis-type A such that "if A were true, C would be a matter of course". This is a backward-chaining, explanation-seeking search, and its permissiveness (Harman) is why it must be paired with a pursuitworthiness filter (Laudan's context of pursuit; Psillos's abduction-then-test).
- Implementable claim (Magnani/Nersessian/Darden): creative abduction is not sampling from a fixed hypothesis set but constructing new hypotheses by (a) schema instantiation from a library of mechanism types, (b) analogical transfer with near domains for repair and distant domains for new concepts (Dunbar), and (c) simulative/visual model-building. These are the three concrete hypothesis-generation operators in the philosophy-of-science literature.
- The bad-lot objection formalises why generation quality bounds inference quality: no amount of evaluation can recover a hypothesis never generated. Lipton's "add the catch-all negation" fix fails because the catch-all explains nothing; so the only remedy is better generation — which reconnects IBE to Boden's evaluation-vs-generation asymmetry and Hills & Bird's fertility condition.
- Nickles's generative justification shows discovery and justification are not fully separable: showing a hypothesis is derivable from accepted results is both a way to find it and a reason to believe it.
