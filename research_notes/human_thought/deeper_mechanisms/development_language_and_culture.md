# Development, Language, Inner Speech, Cultural Evolution and Comparative Innovation: Where New Ideas Come From

Scope note: this round covers developmental, linguistic, cultural-evolutionary and comparative evidence on the sources of new ideas. Adult creativity networks, mind wandering, insight, semantic memory and practical levers were covered in the earlier report and are not re-researched here. Method note: the shared web-search budget ran out after the first batch of searches, so most evidence below was gathered by direct fetches of open-access records (PMC, Europe PMC, arXiv, PLOS, institutional news). Where a paper could only be reached as an abstract, numbers are limited to what the abstract reports; this is flagged in the Gaps sections. Work before 2023 is labeled "foundational" where it is included for context.

## Key Question 1: Childhood as exploration (explore-exploit, "high temperature" search, children vs adults on unusual hypotheses, the fourth-grade slump, play, infant curiosity)

### Takeaway
The best 2023–2026 evidence supports a specific version of Gopnik's claim: children's search is literally "hotter" and less constrained (higher random temperature, more directed exploration, broader and less sequentially dependent semantic search), which lets them find unusual hypotheses adults miss, at the cost of over-exploring on tasks where adults' narrower search is already near-optimal. Development looks like a stochastic-optimization "cooling" schedule that settles by roughly ages 8–13, and infant and preschool curiosity is tuned to information gain and learning progress rather than to novelty per se.

### Cited Findings

Foundational framing and original child-vs-adult results (pre-2023, for context)
- Gopnik's explore-exploit account: the long, protected human childhood "allows an early period of broad hypothesis search and exploration" before goal-directed exploitation; the abstract states "young human learners are highly exploratory." — [Gopnik 2020, Phil Trans R Soc B 375(1803), PMC7293160](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC7293160)
- Lucas, Bridgers, Griffiths & Gopnik (2014, Cognition) tested 106 preschoolers (4–5 years) and 170 undergraduates on a "blicket" machine that worked by an unusual (conjunctive) rule; children were more likely to find the unusual rule while adults kept favoring the familiar one-block-at-a-time rule even as evidence accumulated. The pattern depends on children having weaker priors, not on children "reasoning better" in general. — [Berkeley News coverage of Lucas et al. 2014](https://news.berkeley.edu/?p=39876); [Altmetric record for the paper](https://www.altmetric.com/details/2167949)
- Gopnik et al. (2017, PNAS 114(30):7892–7899): two studies on learning an unusual physical or social causal relation. In the physical domain, preschoolers outperformed school-aged children, who outperformed adolescents and adults; older learners were less willing to adopt an unfamiliar hypothesis supported by new evidence. In the social domain "both preschoolers and adolescents are actually the most flexible learners." — [Gopnik et al. 2017, PMC5544286](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC5544286)
- Cross-cultural generality: Wente et al. (2019, Child Development 90(3):859–875; N = 435) compared low-income Peruvian 4–5-year-olds and adults, low-income US Head Start preschoolers, and middle-class US children; children in all groups learned both specific causal relations and abstract causal principles, with Peruvian children and adults performing much like middle-class US participants and low-SES US children showing some differences. — [Wente et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12943)
- Liquin & Gopnik (2022, Cognition 218:104940) used an approach-avoid "learning trap" task across four studies: preschoolers and early school-aged children explored more than adults and learned the true environment structure better; in Study 3 children explored more even though both children and adults predicted exploration would be costly; in Study 4, children given adult-like evidence learned like adults and adults given child-like evidence learned like children, showing the learning advantage is caused by the evidence exploration generates. — [Liquin & Gopnik 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2021.104940)
- Blanco & Sloutsky (2021, Developmental Science 24(2):e13026): systematic switching (exploration) dominates young children's choices even though the cognitive control usually associated with systematic exploration develops slowly; uncertainty shapes children's choices in a more complex way than a simple effect. — [Blanco & Sloutsky 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.13026)

2023–2026 quantitative tests of the "cooling" account
- Giron, Ciranka, Schulz, van den Bos, Ruggeri, Meder & Wu (2023, Nature Human Behaviour 7(11):1955–1967), N = 281, ages 5–55 (mean 14.5, SD 8.6), spatially correlated 8×8 bandit with 25 choices per round, seven age groups of ~30–50. Reward rose with age (r = 0.51, P < 0.001); unique options sampled per round fell with age (Kendall τ = −0.33, P < 0.001); the two youngest groups (5–6, 7–8) showed decaying learning curves interpreted as over-exploration. In a Gaussian-process UCB model, three parameters changed rapidly in childhood then plateaued (changepoint regression, 95% CIs): random temperature τ decreased with changepoint at 7.7 years [6.9, 8.8]; directed (uncertainty-seeking) exploration β decreased with changepoint at 9.1 years [7.4, 11.4]; generalization λ increased with changepoint at 12.7 years [7.7, 19.8]. Younger participants' parameter estimates were more heterogeneous, older participants' converged. The human trajectory most closely matched stochastic hill-climbing with fast cooling; neither simulated annealing nor hill climbing reliably beat adults aged 25–55 (P = 0.68 and 0.45), and humans settled at lower λ and β than the algorithms (both P < 0.001). Data are cross-sectional and WEIRD. — [Giron et al. 2023, PMC10663152](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10663152/)
- Bonawitz, Walker, Abbott, Griffiths & Gopnik (preprint, 2026): participants generated sequential guesses about a novel causal relation, and the "distance" between successive guesses was measured; adults showed stronger dependencies between sequential guesses and generated less diverse options than preschoolers, which the authors interpret as preschoolers searching semantic networks in a broader, more variable way with implications for hypothesis generation. Abstract gives no numbers. — [Bonawitz et al. 2026 PsyArXiv](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/qpj6n_v2)
- Ruggeri, Stanciu, Pelz, Gopnik & Schulz (2024, Developmental Science 27(1):e13411): across three studies, children aged 24–56 months searched behind doors for a hidden object; they persisted longer when uncertainty about the hidden object (and thus information gained per action) was higher, supporting information gain as an intrinsic reward. — [Ruggeri et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.13411)
- Yiu, Allen, Ginosar & Gopnik (2026, Phil Trans R Soc A 384(2320):20250003) propose "empowerment" (an intrinsic reward maximizing mutual information between actions and outcomes) as a bridge between Bayesian causal learning and reinforcement learning and test how children and adults use controllability and variability cues to infer causal relations and design interventions (no numbers in abstract). — [Yiu et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.2025.0003)
- Goddu, Yiu & Gopnik (2025, Cognition 254:105959): 24–30-month-old toddlers and 3-year-olds inferred relational causal rules (e.g., same/different) from a mechanistically opaque machine after a handful of trials and applied them to novel problems. — [Goddu et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2024.105959)
- Children vs. AI as a window on innovation: Yiu, Kosoy & Gopnik (2024, Perspectives on Psychological Science 19(5):874–883) argue LLMs and vision-language models are "cultural technologies" and imitation engines; they test whether models can discover new tools and novel causal structures and contrast them with children, concluding that innovation of the kind young children show may need more than large-scale language and image data (a 2025 erratum exists). — [Yiu, Kosoy & Gopnik 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/17456916231201401)
- Kosoy, Reagan, Lai, Gopnik & Krettek Cobb (arXiv, 2023) adapted classic developmental experiments for LaMDA: its responses in social understanding were child-like, but in early object/action understanding, theory of mind and especially causal reasoning they diverged strongly from children, which the authors attribute to domains requiring self-initiated real-world exploration. — [Kosoy et al. 2023, arXiv:2305.11243](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11243)
- A 2024 arXiv position paper frames the same idea for AI: the less-informed learner can launch an expanded (high-temperature) probe with limited evidence, whereas a precocious learner initiates a constricted (low-temperature) search and fine-tunes. — [Metalearning-Informed Competence in Children, arXiv:2401.01001](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.01001)

Play and pretend as exploration
- Chu & Schulz (2023, Open Mind 7:294–317): in four experiments with 4–5-year-olds, children inferred playful intent from observed irrational actions (Exp. 1) and themselves took on unnecessary costs in retrieval (Exp. 2) and search (Exps. 3A–B) tasks while behaving efficiently in instrumental contexts; the authors argue this utility-violating behavior may serve learning in the long run. — [Chu & Schulz 2023](https://doi.org/10.1162/opmi_a_00076)
- Wente, Gopnik, Fernández Flecha, Garcia & Buchsbaum (2022, Phil Trans B): 179 three-to-four-year-olds (62 Peruvian, 57 low-income US, 60 mixed-SES US); children were more causally consistent when pretending than when reasoning counterfactually, and the two measures were correlated; lower-income groups gave fewer causally consistent answers. — [Wente et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2021.0345)
- Counterfactual thinking emerges early: Gautam, Suddendorf & Redshaw (2022, Phil Trans B; N = 160, ages 4–9) found children report more negative emotional shifts when a missed prize was a plausible alternative; Gautam et al. (2023, Child Development; N = 236, ages 4–9) found 4–5-year-olds judge morality by outcomes only, while from age 6 children weigh counterfactual choices. — [Gautam et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2021.0346); [Gautam et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13943)
- Pretend-play training transfers narrowly: Adam et al. (2022, JECP; 70 children aged 4–6, 20 sessions over 10 weeks) improved self-regulation within the activities but did not transfer to self-regulation outside training, cognitive control, or EEG theta. Richard, Clerc-Georgy & Gentaz (2023, Acta Psychologica; 101 trained vs 79 controls, ages 5–6) found gains in emotion comprehension and fewer aggressive responses. — [Adam et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2021.105348); [Richard et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2023.103961)

Infant curiosity: Goldilocks, surprise, learning progress
- Foundational: Kidd, Piantadosi & Aslin (2012, PLOS ONE): 42 infants (mean 7.9 months) in Exp. 1 and 30 (mean 7.6 months) in Exp. 2; using an ideal-learner (Markov Dirichlet-multinomial) model of event complexity (negative log probability) and Cox regression on look-away, look-away probability was U-shaped in complexity: infants disengaged from both highly predictable and highly surprising events, attending most to intermediate complexity. — [Kidd et al. 2012](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0036399)
- Foundational: Stahl & Feigenson (2015, Science 348:91–94): 110 eleven-month-olds learned more about objects that violated expectations, explored them more, and tested hypotheses matched to the violation type (e.g., banging an object that had appeared to pass through a wall); "expectancy violations offer a wedge into the problem of what to learn." — [Stahl & Feigenson 2015](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaa3799)
- Perez & Feigenson (2022, Cognition 218:104942): surprise-induced exploration disappeared when the violation was explained (the wall had a hole); the longer infants looked at the explanation, the more they reversed their preference; replicated in Exp. 3. Preverbal infants both seek and recognize explanations. — [Perez & Feigenson 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2021.104942)
- Cao, Smith-Flores, Zhou, Perez & Feigenson (2025, Cognition 264:106227): across three experiments with 16–19-month-olds, violations of social expectations (a person reacting or choosing in a reversed, surprising way) enhanced learning about the object involved and to some extent about the person. — [Cao et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2025.106227)
- Liu & Feigenson (2026, Infancy 31(4)): three experiments with 36 infants each (17–24 months); infants who saw a person repeatedly express surprise at a box inferred she had seen multiple (novel) objects, whereas repeated happiness did not license that inference. — [Liu & Feigenson 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/infa.70117)
- Poli, Serino, Mars & Hunnius (2020, Science Advances 6(39)): infants' saccade latency, look duration and engagement tracked model-derived surprise, environmental predictability and how much had already been learned, consistent with infants seeking stimuli that maximize learning. — [Poli et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abb5053)
- Poli et al. (2024, Developmental Science): 106 eight-month-olds; faster processing speed predicted shorter habituation, higher model-estimated curiosity predicted stronger dishabituation (only in infants who did not habituate). — [Poli et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.13460)
- Poli, Meyer, Mars & Hunnius (2025, Child Development): 102 four-year-olds in a touchscreen game freely switched among characters; Bayesian modeling showed preference for options with higher novelty and higher learning progress, and sensitivity to learning progress correlated with better learning. — [Poli et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.14158)
- de Boer, Poli, Meyer, Mars & Hunnius (2026, Developmental Science): longitudinal n = 60; 8-month-olds whose attention tracked information gain more strongly had higher IQ at 3.5 years. — [de Boer et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.70090)
- A null: Bazhydai et al. (2026, Developmental Science) found no retention advantage for self-directed (curiosity-driven) selection of word-learning opportunities in 20–23-month-olds; all conditions learned above chance. — [Bazhydai et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1111/desc.70101)
- Ziv et al. (2026, Developmental Cognitive Neuroscience): fMRI in 5–8-year-olds (n = 51 imaging, 60 behavior); higher self-reported state curiosity predicted better learning and greater activation in inferior frontal gyrus, thalamus and putamen. — [Ziv et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dcn.2026.101687)
- Comparative curiosity: Sánchez-Amaro & Rossano (2023, PLOS ONE): most great apes explored uncertain options only after better alternatives appeared, whereas children explored them earlier. Lewis et al. (2025, Proc R Soc B; chimpanzees n = 27, children 4–6 n = 94) found both species prefer watching social interactions and male chimpanzees and young children paid a material cost for social information. — [Sánchez-Amaro & Rossano 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0285946); [Lewis et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2024.2242)

Fourth-grade slump and developmental trajectories of divergent thinking
- No 2023–2026 study located directly charts a within-person divergent-thinking trajectory across childhood to adulthood. A cohort (not within-person) decline: Bakhiet et al. (2022, Acta Psychologica 231:103797) found Torrance scores of 8–12-year-olds in Khartoum private schools fell between 2005 and 2018 on fluency, flexibility, originality and the overall index. — [Bakhiet et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103797)
- Tomás, Bajo & Marful (2026, PLOS ONE 21(3):e0341294) compared second graders, fifth graders and young adults: episodic specificity induction improved episodic recall but did not transfer to divergent thinking; the control group in Exp. 1 showed a significant rise in fluency and flexibility. — [Tomás et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0341294)
- Executive subcomponents dissociate in adolescence: Fischer et al. (2026, BMC Psychology; 344 adolescents) found set-shifting (WCST) predicted both fluency and originality while task-switching predicted lower originality. — [Fischer et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-04584-5)
- Zheng, Denervaud & Durrleman (2022, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience; 92 participants aged 4–18, structural MRI in 75): bilingualism advantaged convergent but not divergent thinking, and the advantage weakened with age. — [Zheng et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2022.1058803)
- Measurement caution relevant to all child divergent-thinking work: Barbot, Van den Noortgate & Said-Metwaly (2026, Journal of Intelligence 14(6):100) found the serial-order effect in the Alternate Uses Task is robust at group level but explains at most 3.5% of variance and shows no stable individual differences (ICCs < 0.31). — [Barbot et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/jintelligence14060100)

### Inferences
- The field has moved from "children are better Bayesians" to a mechanistic account in which three things fall with age: random noise in choice, uncertainty-directed sampling, and the heterogeneity of strategies across individuals. The adult advantage on most tasks and the child advantage on unusual-hypothesis tasks are two faces of the same cooling schedule.
- The cooling appears to be settled early: temperature by ~8, directed exploration by ~9, generalization by ~13 (Giron et al.). If a "fourth-grade slump" (around age 9–10) exists, this timetable is a plausible mechanism for it, but no 2023–2026 study I found tests that link directly.
- Infant work has converged on information gain / learning progress as the quantity curiosity tracks (Kidd's U-shape, Stahl & Feigenson's hypothesis-matched exploration, Poli's learning-progress preference, de Boer's longitudinal link to later IQ). The Bazhydai null suggests self-direction per se does not guarantee better learning; it is the informational structure that matters.
- Play's contribution to idea generation is best supported as "licensed cost-insensitive exploration" (Chu & Schulz) rather than as a trainable skill: pretend-play training transfers to within-activity regulation and socio-emotional measures but not to general cognitive control.

### Gaps
- Direct independent replications of Lucas et al. (2014) and Gopnik et al. (2017) published 2023–2026 could not be located with the tools available; the closest are extensions from the same group (Liquin & Gopnik 2022; Bonawitz et al. 2026 preprint) and conceptual replications in Peru/Head Start (Wente et al. 2019).
- The fourth-grade slump meta-analysis (Said-Metwaly et al., Educational Psychology Review, 2021, DOI 10.1007/s10648-020-09547-9) is not indexed in Europe PMC and the publisher page could not be fetched, so its k, N and conclusions are not reported here; its status should be verified before citing.
- Full text of the Bonawitz et al. 2026 preprint was not retrievable (only the record), so sample sizes and effect sizes are missing.

## Key Question 2: Language and thought (Fedorenko, Piantadosi & Gibson 2024 and responses; aphasia and fMRI; linguistic relativity and label feedback; what language adds)

### Takeaway
The 2024–2026 evidence from Fedorenko's group is now unusually direct: the language network is not engaged during logical, semantic or mathematical reasoning, severely aphasic patients reason normally, and even deductive reasoning uses regions distinct from both language and the multiple-demand network. The main counter-position (Lupyan, Boroditsky, Clark) does not dispute this but argues that language augments cognition by making representations more categorical, more compact and more shareable, and that it carries culturally accumulated abstractions, so a thinker without language is possible but thinks differently.

### Cited Findings
- Core claim: Fedorenko, Piantadosi & Gibson (2024, Nature 630:575–586) argue that in modern humans language is primarily a tool for communication, citing a double dissociation between language and thought and features of language that look optimized for communication; language is not required for complex or symbolic thought but is a powerful means of passing on cultural knowledge and reflects rather than produces cognitive sophistication. — [Fedorenko et al. 2024, Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1038/s41586-024-07522-w&resultType=core&format=json); [MIT News summary](https://news.mit.edu/2024/what-is-language-for-0703)
- Evidence lines as described by the authors: individually localized language networks show little or no response during sudoku and theory-of-mind reasoning ("Your language system is basically silent when you do all sorts of thinking"); severe aphasics can solve math problems, play chess and plan future events ("They can do all the things that they could do before their injury"); people with intellectual disability or neuropsychiatric disorders that impair reasoning do not necessarily have impaired basic language (reverse dissociation); cross-linguistic analyses of dozens of spoken and signed languages find common words are short, dependent words sit close together, and languages are robust to signal distortion, which would be pointless if language's main job were internal thought. — [MIT News 2024](https://news.mit.edu/2024/what-is-language-for-0703)
- Foundational detail (Fedorenko & Varley 2016, Ann NY Acad Sci): three severely aphasic patients with extensive left-hemisphere damage solved addition, subtraction, multiplication and division including fractions and bracketed expressions while severely agrammatic; a severely agrammatic man performed well on complex causal reasoning; severely agrammatic patients inferred true and false beliefs; global aphasics succeeded as both sender and receiver in a nonverbal communication game; the composer Shebalin composed work judged comparable to his earlier work after a stroke left him severely aphasic. fMRI: language regions (top 10% of voxels, sentences > nonwords) respond at or below fixation baseline to arithmetic, show no response to working-memory and inhibitory-control tasks, respond weakly and non-overlappingly to logic, and show little response to music; algebraic syntax activates parietal rather than frontotemporal regions. Counter-evidence acknowledged: Baldo et al. found Wisconsin Card Sorting impairments in aphasics, and Reverberi et al. found deductive deficits with prefrontal lesions and preserved language. — [Fedorenko & Varley 2016, PMC4874898](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4874898/)
- New 2026 test: Kean, Fung, Jaggers, Chen, Rule, Benn, Tenenbaum, Piantadosi, Varley & Fedorenko (2026, PNAS 123(28):e2520095123), "Evidence from formal logical reasoning reveals that the language of thought is not natural language": fMRI in healthy adults showed the language network is not engaged during logical reasoning; patients with extensive language-area lesions and severe aphasia performed normally on logic tasks; inductive reasoning recruited the domain-general multiple-demand network, while deductive reasoning used regions distinct from both language and multiple-demand networks. — [Kean et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2520095123)
- Semantic reasoning outside language: Ivanova et al. (bioRxiv 2025; 41 participants, 49 sessions, three fMRI experiments) found semantic-task areas in left lateral prefrontal cortex, left temporo-parietal cortex and right cerebellum that respond to both sentences and pictures and are spatially and functionally distinct from adjacent language-selective areas. — [Ivanova et al. 2025 preprint](https://doi.org/10.64898/2025.12.07.692873)
- Division of labor: Gao et al. (2026, Imaging Neuroscience; 52 participants, six task conditions) found the language network responds robustly to sentences across tasks, the multiple-demand network responds to sentences and nonwords when a task is present, stimulus information is more decodable from the language network and task information from the multiple-demand network. — [Gao et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1162/imag.a.1283)
- The language network is stable and selective at scale: Shain & Fedorenko (2026, Nature Communications; 1,957 sessions, 1,199 brains) recover a left-dominant frontotemporal language network from task-agnostic connectivity; Wolna et al. (2026, J Neurosci; 772 participants) find an extended language-selective network under 5% of gray-matter volume; Ozernov-Palchik et al. (2026, Nature Communications; 273 children aged 4–16, 107 adults) find adult-like left lateralization by age 4; Olson et al. (2026 preprint; 80 children, 10 adult musicians) find language areas selective for language over music with no age change. — [Shain & Fedorenko 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-75745-8); [Wolna et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0638-25.2026); [Ozernov-Palchik et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-72916-5); [Olson et al. 2026 preprint](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.09.03.749155)
- Responses and the "augmentation" counter-position: Lupyan, Gentry & Zettersten (2026, Perspectives on Psychological Science 21(1):115–120), "How Important Is Language for Human-Like Intelligence?", argue language provides compact representations for abstract concepts and carries culturally accumulated abstractions, so a powerful learner exposed to language can recover many conceptual and causal structures. — [Lupyan et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/17456916251398539)
- Pleyer, Perlman, Lupyan, de Reus & Raviv (2026, TICS 30(7):611–631) revisit Hockett's design features and conclude language is multimodal and supports semantic, pragmatic and social inference and categorization, not just communication. — [Pleyer et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2025.10.004)
- Foundational linguistic-relativity-as-modulation evidence: the label-feedback hypothesis (Lupyan 2012, Frontiers in Psychology) holds that words transiently modulate perception/categorization via top-down feedback, explaining why language effects are deep yet disrupted by verbal interference. Cited effects: Russian speakers' categorical blue perception vanished under verbal interference (Winawer et al. 2007); Greek speakers show larger early visual mismatch negativity (~160 ms) for color changes crossing their lexical boundary (Thierry et al. 2009); hearing a redundant label ("five") sped detection of 2s vs 5s in 100-ms displays but only if the label preceded by less than ~1600 ms; labels increased d′ for masked objects and made CFS-suppressed stimuli visible; aphasic patients' naming scores predicted dimension-specific categorization deficits; labeled novel categories were learned faster and retained better (16 "aliens"). — [Lupyan 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00054/full)
- Lupyan, Abdel Rahman, Boroditsky & Clark (2020, TICS 24(11):930–944): behavioral and electrophysiological evidence that language affects recognition, discrimination and detection; "a consistent finding is that language causes us to perceive in a more categorical way," which follows from perception being interactive and predictive. — [Lupyan et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2020.08.005)
- 2024–2025 label effects: Suffill, van Paridon & Lupyan (2024, Open Mind 8:950–971) found participants who heard one of two nonsense labels during pre-exposure sorted novel shapes more categorically, increasing representational alignment between people ("Mind Melds"). Liu, van Paridon & Lupyan (2025, Communications Psychology 3:60) showed word-embedding projections predict color-adjective associations reported by blind and sighted English speakers, with fiction-trained embeddings outperforming GPT-4, i.e., language alone carries recoverable perceptual-conceptual structure. — [Suffill et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1162/opmi_a_00153); [Liu et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44271-025-00230-9)
- Culture shapes "thinking skills" tasks: Kroupin, Davis, Burdett, Cuata, Hartley & Henrich (2025, PNAS 122(27)) compared schooled UK children aged 5–18 with rural Namibian children with little or no schooling and found profound, sometimes qualitative, differences on standard executive-function tasks, suggesting these tasks measure culturally specific skills. — [Kroupin et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2407955122)
- Fedorenko's group also engages the LLM debate: de Varda, D'Elia, Kean, Lampinen & Fedorenko published three PNAS replies in 2026 defending the claim that LLM reasoning traces track the cost of human thinking (titles only retrieved). — [Reply to Vankov et al.](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2603574123); [Reply to Hu](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2606498123); [Reply to Dujmović](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2536153123)
- Press commentary (Substack, author unverified) treats the Nature paper as evidence against language-scaling routes to AGI and reports an interview with Fedorenko; this is opinion, not peer review. — [Build Cognitive Resonance, "People thinking without speaking, part three"](https://buildcognitiveresonance.substack.com/p/people-thinking-without-speaking-c19)

### Inferences
- For idea generation specifically, the data imply that the generative machinery (inductive generalization in multiple-demand cortex; deductive machinery elsewhere; semantic combination in non-language regions) does not need language online. Language's contribution is upstream (installing categories and abstractions from culture) and downstream (compressing, stabilizing and transmitting ideas).
- The two camps are compatible: Fedorenko et al. deny that language is the medium of thought; Lupyan et al. claim language changes what representations are available and how categorical they are. Both predict that an agent with a rich non-linguistic learner plus exposure to language gains most of the benefit, which matches the children-vs-LLM contrast in Question 1 (LLMs match children on socially/linguistically transmitted knowledge, not on causal discovery).
- The Kroupin et al. result cautions that many "thinking skills" measured in developmental creativity research are themselves culturally installed, likely through schooling and language.

### Gaps
- No formal peer-reviewed Matters Arising or rebuttal to Fedorenko, Piantadosi & Gibson (2024) in Nature was located; the strongest published counter-statements found are the Lupyan et al. 2026 and Pleyer et al. 2026 pieces, which are positional rather than direct rebuttals.
- Sample sizes for the Kean et al. 2026 fMRI and aphasia samples were not in the abstract.
- No 2023–2026 Boroditsky-authored empirical paper was retrieved; linguistic relativity is represented here through Lupyan and co-authors.

## Key Question 3: Inner speech (dialogic thinking, descriptive experience sampling, unsymbolized thinking, 2023–2026 neuroimaging/decoding, anendophasia)

### Takeaway
Inner speech is far from universal or constant: experience sampling puts it at roughly a quarter of waking moments with enormous individual variation, and much thinking is "unsymbolized." People who report little inner speech show selective deficits (verbal working memory, rhyme judgment) but intact task switching and perceptual categorization, and aphasia data show a double dissociation between inner speech and executive function. 2025 intracranial work shows inner speech is decodable from motor cortex and separated from attempted speech by a "motor-intent" dimension. The weight of evidence is that inner speech is a tool that supports some reasoning and self-regulation functions rather than the medium of thought.

### Cited Findings
- Foundational phenomenology: Heavey & Hurlburt (2008, Consciousness and Cognition 17(3):798–810) sampled 10 random moments from each of 30 students with DES; inner speech, inner seeing, unsymbolized thinking, feelings and sensory awareness each occurred in about one quarter of moments, with wide between-person variability; more frequent inner speech was linked to lower psychological distress. — [Heavey & Hurlburt 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2007.12.006)
- Hurlburt, Heavey & Kelsey (2013, Consciousness and Cognition 22(4):1477–1494) describe inner speaking as present in many but not all moments, usually in one's own voice with normal intonation, with large individual differences in frequency and a range of variations; they attribute discrepancies with questionnaire estimates to method. — [Hurlburt et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.concog.2013.10.003)
- Review of frequency, forms and functions (Alderson-Day & Fernyhough 2015, Psychological Bulletin): DES suggests inner speech in roughly 20–25% of samples versus ~75% "interior monologue" in older thought-sampling (Klinger & Cox 1987); Fernyhough's model distinguishes expanded (phonologically detailed, turn-taking) from condensed (near pure meaning) inner speech, with condensed as default and stress or challenge shifting to expanded; on the Varieties of Inner Speech Questionnaire, evaluative/motivational speech was endorsed by 82.5%, dialogic by 77.2%, condensed by 36.1%, other people's voices by 25.8%. Functional evidence: articulatory suppression removes word-length and phonological-similarity effects; the phonological similarity effect appears around age 7; inner speech aids task-switch preparation when cues are sparse (Emerson & Miyake 2003); planning evidence is mixed; inner speech does not appear necessary for logical reasoning. Neural: LIFG (pars opercularis) in overt and silent articulation, STG in monitoring, SMA/premotor in articulatory planning; many elicitation studies lack behavioral checks that inner speech was produced. — [Alderson-Day & Fernyhough 2015, PMC4538954](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4538954/)
- Dialogic inner speech network (foundational): Alderson-Day et al. (2016, SCAN) found dialogic versus monologic inner-speech generation recruited a widespread bilateral network including left and right superior temporal gyri, precuneus, posterior cingulate and left inferior and medial frontal gyri (theory-of-mind-like regions). — [Alderson-Day et al. 2016, PMC4692319](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4692319)
- Fernyhough & Borghi (2023, TICS 27(12):1180–1193) review inner speech as a functional language process that benefits cognition across domains, is embodied with varied subjective qualities, is worth modeling in artificial systems, and may become decodable by BCIs; open challenges are individual differences and mapping subtypes to functions. Borghi & Fernyhough (2023, Phil Trans B 378:20210371) propose inner speech supports learning and use of abstract concepts via "inner social metacognition." — [Fernyhough & Borghi 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2023.08.014); [Borghi & Fernyhough 2023](https://doi.org/10.1098/rstb.2021.0371)
- Neurodiversity: Alderson-Day & Pearson (2023, Cortex 168:193–202) review inner speech across autistic, neurodivergent and deaf populations and argue the inner-speech–cognition link is more complex than assumed. — [Alderson-Day & Pearson 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cortex.2023.08.008)
- Anendophasia: Nedergaard & Lupyan (2024, Psychological Science 35(7):780–797) selected adults from the Internal Representations Questionnaire verbal factor (low: below 3.5, bottom 16%; high: above 4.25, top 40%), final N = 93 (46 low, 47 high), matched on age, gender, education, dyslexia and first language. Low-inner-speech adults performed worse on verbal working memory (phonologically or orthographically similar word lists) and rhyme judgments from pictures; no group differences in task switching (e.g., alternating addition/subtraction) or categorical effects on perceptual judgments (cat/dog silhouettes). Some low-inner-speech participants reported compensatory strategies such as tapping fingers for task types. Effect sizes were not reported in the sources retrieved. — [Nedergaard & Lupyan 2024, Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1177/09567976241243004&resultType=core&format=json); [PsyPost report](https://www.psypost.org/anendophasia-scientists-examine-the-cognitive-impact-of-life-without-an-inner-voice); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/silent-inner-world-anendophasia)
- Critique: Lind (2025, Psychological Science 36(9):765–767) argues the data show less inner speech, not none, and that coining "anendophasia" for total absence overreaches. — [Lind 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976251335583)
- Hurlburt (2026, Psychological Science) replies to both, arguing anendophasia is an experiential phenomenon requiring fidelity-based (DES-style) investigation rather than questionnaire constructs, and distinguishing "completely" from "mostly" anendophasic individuals, suggesting the latter are probably common. — [Hurlburt 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976251413525)
- Inner speech and executive function in aphasia: Fama, McCall, DeMarco & Turkeltaub (2024, Neuropsychologia 204:108997), 59 adults with chronic post-stroke aphasia; inner speech showed tentative links to most executive measures (not response inhibition), and a double dissociation: many participants had intact executive function despite poor inner speech, and vice versa. — [Fama et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2024.108997)
- Intracranial decoding: Kunz et al. (2025, Cell 188(17):4658–4673), multi-unit recordings from four BCI participants; inner (imagined) speech is robustly represented in motor cortex and imagined sentences were decoded in real time; inner-speech activity is highly correlated with attempted speech but separated by a "motor-intent" neural dimension; aspects of free-form inner speech were decodable during sequence recall and counting; the authors propose safeguards against unintended decoding of private inner speech. — [Kunz et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2025.06.015)
- EEG signatures: Whitford et al. (2025, eNeuro; 129 healthy participants) found inner-speech content (Match vs Mismatch with a heard syllable) is distinguished by evoked gamma and theta/alpha oscillations, with N1 suppressed on Match and P2 suppressed on Mismatch, consistent with a corollary-discharge mechanism; in schizophrenia spectrum disorders (Whitford, Chung et al. 2025, Schizophrenia Bulletin; 55 hallucinating, 44 non-hallucinating, 43 controls) controls showed N1 suppression on Match while hallucinating patients showed the opposite. — [Whitford et al. 2025 eNeuro](https://doi.org/10.1523/eneuro.0297-24.2025); [Whitford, Chung et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1093/schbul/sbaf167)
- Meta-analysis of neural mechanisms: Pratts, Pobric & Yao (2023, NeuroImage 282:120399) used ALE meta-analysis to test a dual-mechanism framework in which inner speech arises from corollary discharge in production regions or perceptual simulation in perception regions. A narrative review of 83 studies (Dahò & Monzani 2025, Cognitive Neuropsychology 42:1–21) reports dialogal inner speech recruits theory-of-mind networks and spontaneous inner speech engages Heschl's gyrus. — [Pratts et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2023.120399); [Dahò & Monzani 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/02643294.2025.2527983)
- Form matters: Kreiner & Eviatar (2024, Physics of Life Reviews 51:231–242) propose inner speech simulates acoustic and prosodic structure, giving form to thought and supporting attention, memory, emotion and self-regulation. — [Kreiner & Eviatar 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plrev.2024.10.006)

### Inferences
- The ~25% DES figure plus the anendophasia and aphasia dissociations imply that for most people, most of the time, thinking (including idea generation) proceeds without a verbal stream; inner speech is recruited selectively, especially for phonological maintenance and under load or stress (expanded form).
- Dialogic inner speech recruits social-cognitive networks, which fits the view that self-talk is a tool for taking alternative perspectives on one's own ideas (internalized dialogue), a plausible mechanism for self-evaluation during idea generation, though no 2023–2026 study directly linked dialogic inner speech to creative output.
- The motor-cortex decodability of inner speech supports Vygotskian internalization (inner speech as inhibited articulation) and shows that "unsymbolized thinking" and inner speech are empirically separable states, not just reporting styles.

### Gaps
- No retrieved 2023–2026 study directly tests inner speech (or its absence) against idea-generation or divergent-thinking performance; the anendophasia tasks were memory, rhyme, switching and categorization.
- Effect sizes for Nedergaard & Lupyan (2024) were not available from accessible sources (publisher page blocked).
- No 2023–2026 study quantifying "unsymbolized thinking" frequency beyond the 2008 baseline was found.
- Aphantasia's effect on thinking was not researched in this round (the brief grouped it with anendophasia; sources retrieved covered only inner speech).

## Key Question 4: Cultural evolution of ideas (cumulative culture, collective brain, recombination, adjacent possible / Heaps' law, large-scale studies of scientific novelty and the "declining disruptiveness" debate)

### Takeaway
Across experiments, models and bibliometrics, new ideas at the population level come from recombination under conditions that preserve diversity and allow selective learning: partially connected or large populations with external records, teams and outsiders who bring surprising content–context combinations, and co-located teams for conceptual (tacit) work. The adjacent-possible/Heaps'-law tradition has been extended to higher-order novelties (new combinations), and the claim that papers and patents are becoming less disruptive has survived its main artefact critique as of 2026, though a minority of analyses still contest it.

### Cited Findings

Collective brain and cumulative culture (foundational and 2022–2026)
- Muthukrishna & Henrich (2016, Phil Trans B 371:20150192) argue innovation emerges from cultural learning in social networks ("Our societies and social networks act as collective brains"), name serendipity, recombination and incremental improvement as the main sources of innovation, and propose sociality, transmission fidelity and cultural variance as determinants of innovation rates; preliminary evidence that languages with more speakers are more transmission-efficient. — [Muthukrishna & Henrich 2016, PMC4780534](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC4780534)
- Thompson, van Opheusden, Sumers & Griffiths (2022, Science 376:95–98): 3,450 participants, 20 populations, 12 generations, sorting an unknown sequence; known sorting algorithms emerged; when learners could choose whom to learn from, complex high-performing algorithms persisted, and without that choice populations converged on simpler, more transmissible algorithms. — [Thompson et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.abn0915)
- Walker & Fay (2026, Scientific Reports 16:10090): two experiments (combined N = 941) on a virtual arrowhead technology; the population-size effect on cumulative culture depended on attention filtering (focusing on high-payoff solutions), and larger populations enhanced cumulative culture when participants could use an external record (diagrams/notes) that supports payoff comparison and recombination. — [Walker & Fay 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-40973-x)
- Osiurak, Claidière & Federico (2023, TICS 27(1):30–42) argue that reproducing others' tool actions requires causal understanding, challenging the copying-vs-reasoning dichotomy and the existence of a separate "copying ability"; causal understanding is a key driver of cumulative technological culture. — [Osiurak et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2022.09.024)
- Paige & Perreault (2024, PNAS 121(26):e2319175121): across 3.3 million years of stone-tool manufacturing sequences, technologies became significantly more complex than achievable without cumulative culture only after ~600 kya. — [Paige & Perreault 2024](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2319175121)
- Brinkmann et al. (2023, Nature Human Behaviour 7:1855–1868), "Machine culture," argue intelligent machines alter the variation, transmission and selection stages of cultural evolution (recommenders changing social learning, chatbots as cultural models, machines contributing cultural traits such as game strategies and scientific results). — [Brinkmann et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-023-01742-2)
- Czaplicka, Baumann & Rahwan (2025, J R Soc Interface 22:20240686): model combining network social learning with algorithmic mediation; mediation benefits grow as networks become less dense, and both together work best. — [Czaplicka et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2024.0686)

Adjacent possible, Heaps' law, higher-order novelties
- Foundational: Tria, Loreto, Servedio & Strogatz (2014, Scientific Reports; arXiv 2013) generalize Pólya's urn with a triggering mechanism (one novelty makes others more likely) as a quantitative version of Kauffman's adjacent possible; it predicts Heaps'-law novelty rates and Zipf-like distributions and was tested on Wikipedia edits, tag emergence, word sequences in texts, and new-song listening. — [Tria et al., arXiv:1310.1953](https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.1953)
- Loreto et al. (2016) extend this to walks on an expanding graph where visiting a new node reveals previously unforeseeable regions. A 2022 critique notes the adjacent possible's size cannot be estimated at a point in time unless everything in it is later discovered, and contrasts urn models with recombination-counting theories predicting super-exponential innovation. — [Loreto et al. 2016, arXiv:1701.00994](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1701.00994); [arXiv:2208.00907](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2208.00907)
- Di Bona, Bellina, De Marzo, Petralia, Iacopini & Latora (2025, Nature Communications 16) define higher-order novelties (first co-occurrence of two or more elements) and higher-order Heaps' exponents; processes with the same standard Heaps' exponent can diverge at higher orders, and a random walk on an evolving network reproduces the patterns. — [Di Bona et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-55115-y)
- Loreto's group on technology space: Straccamore, Loreto & Gravino (2023, Scientific Reports 13) find political geography drove patent-based innovation diffusion among metropolitan areas until about two decades ago and identify two main pathways in a competitiveness–diversification space (patents 1980–2014); Straccamore et al. (2023, Scientific Reports 13) link coherent technological diversification of cities to GDP-per-capita growth. Lo Sardo, Gravino, Cuskley & Loreto (2023, PLOS ONE) track exploration (planning) vs exploitation (translation) cycles in 61 researchers' essays and find exploratory turning points become rarer as drafts near completion. — [Straccamore et al. 2023a](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-48342-8); [Straccamore et al. 2023b](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-30649-1); [Lo Sardo et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0283628)

Recombination and novelty in science and technology
- Foundational: Uzzi, Mukherjee, Stringer & Jones (2013, Science 342:468–472), 17.9 million papers: highest-impact work rests on highly conventional combinations of prior work plus an intrusion of unusual combinations; papers with this mix were twice as likely to be highly cited; teams were 37.7% more likely than solo authors to insert novel combinations. — [Uzzi et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1240474)
- Shi & Evans (2023, Nature Communications 14:1641): using a hypergraph model over tens of millions of papers and patents to predict realized combinations of contents (keywords) and contexts (cited journals), surprise predicted top-10% citation impact, and surprising advances tend to arise when scientists from one field publish problem-solving results for an audience in a distant field (outsiders). — [Shi & Evans 2023, PMC10039062](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC10039062)
- Chu & Evans (2021, PNAS 118(41)): in large fields, citations concentrate on already well-cited papers, top-cited lists ossify, new papers rarely become highly cited and seldom disrupt; proposed mechanisms are limited absorptive capacity for novel ideas and competition among too many new ideas preventing focused building on any one. — [Chu & Evans 2021](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2021636118)
- Lin, Frey & Wu (2023, Nature 623:987–991): ~20 million articles and 4 million patent applications over 50 years; remote (cross-city) collaboration has risen, yet across fields, periods and team sizes remote teams are less likely than on-site teams to produce breakthrough (disruptive) work; remote members mainly handle late-stage technical tasks with codified knowledge and less often collaborate on conceptual tasks where knowledge is tacit. — [Lin, Frey & Wu 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06767-1)
- Park, Leahey & Funk (2023, Nature 613:138–144): 45 million papers and 3.9 million patents over six decades; the CD index shows papers and patents are increasingly less likely to break with the past, across all fields and multiple citation- and text-based measures; linked to a narrowing in the use of prior knowledge; not explained by changes in quality, citation practices or field-specific factors. — [Park, Leahey & Funk 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-05543-x)
- Critique: Holst, Algaba, Tori, Wenmackers & Ginis (arXiv 2024) argue the decline is an artefact of database entries with zero references (CD index = 1) whose relative decline drives the trend, that regression adjustment fails to control for them, and that Monte-Carlo simulations with random citation behavior reproduce the decline once these outliers are retained. — [Holst et al. 2024, arXiv:2402.14583](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.14583)
- Rebuttal: Park, Leahey & Funk (arXiv 2025, v2 March 2026; and a Nature reply published August 2026) re-ran the critics' own dataset, metric, exclusion criteria and model; large significant declines remain for papers and patents (p < 0.001) in the critics' own regression (their Table S1); ~20% of the critics' sample is non-research content (at least 2.8 million editorials/obituaries/comments, 1.5 million books and proceedings, 254,000 reviews), which fell from ~40% to 8% of the sample between 1945 and 2010 and can itself generate the zero-reference trend; the remaining decline is described as "equal to the gap between an average paper and one by a Nobel laureate"; declining disruptiveness has been documented in roughly 100 independent studies. — [Park et al. 2025/2026, arXiv:2503.00184](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.00184); [INSEAD summary of the Nature reply](https://www.insead.edu/news/insead-led-research-team-refutes-criticism-landmark-finding-decline-disruptive-science)
- Inventory: an arXiv review (2026) catalogs 105 studies across papers, patents, products, legal cases, music and visual art using citation, text, firm-displacement, product-similarity and audio/visual embedding measures; evidence is "largely consistent in direction" (decline) with notable exceptions such as domain-specific rebounds and predictable field-lifecycle variation. A separate critique by Petersen et al. (2025, J Informetrics) found trends "at the level of noise" after controls in 1995–2015 data, a window after the steepest documented decline. — [arXiv:2602.05140](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.05140)

### Inferences
- The experimental (Thompson; Walker & Fay), observational (Lin et al.) and bibliometric (Uzzi; Shi & Evans) results converge on the same mechanism: novelty requires both access to diverse material and a filter that preserves high-value variants; too little filtering collapses to simple transmissible ideas, too much connectivity homogenizes.
- "External records" (Walker & Fay) and language as a compact code (Lupyan et al., Question 2) are the same lever at different scales: offloading representations lowers the cost of comparison and recombination.
- The disruptiveness decline, if real, is consistent with Chu & Evans's crowding account and with Park et al.'s narrowing of prior-knowledge use: at population scale, more knowledge has reduced rather than increased the rate of unusual recombination. This mirrors the within-person "cooling" story: in both cases search becomes more conventional as the knowledge base grows.

### Gaps
- Full texts for Park et al. 2023, Lin et al. 2023 and Shi & Evans 2023 were not accessible (publisher blocks), so field-level percentage declines and effect sizes beyond abstracts are not reported.
- No 2023–2026 experimental transmission-chain study specifically on children's cumulative culture was located.
- Henrich- or Muthukrishna-authored 2023–2026 work retrieved concerned cultural psychology and WEIRD sampling rather than innovation per se; no updated "collective brain" empirical paper was found.

## Key Question 5: Comparative innovation (crows, primates, cetaceans, octopuses; minimal cognitive ingredients)

### Takeaway
Comparative work points to a narrow set of ingredients for generating new behaviors: a propensity to produce novel motor solutions to physical obstacles (which predicts wild innovation rates and brain size better than associative learning, reversal learning or self-control), neophilia, and the ability to represent and plan over out-of-sight states; what non-human animals largely lack is the social-learning scaffolding and population connectivity that lets isolated innovations accumulate, as shown by chimpanzees who could not innovate a puzzle-box solution in three months but learned it socially within weeks.

### Cited Findings
- Foundational: Reader & Laland (2002, PNAS 99:4436–4441) compiled 533 innovations, 445 social-learning and 607 tool-use reports across primates; all three correlated with relative and absolute "executive" brain volume after controlling for phylogeny and research effort, and innovation and social learning covaried across species, contradicting an individual-vs-social-learning trade-off. — [Reader & Laland 2002, PMC123666](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC123666)
- Audet, Couture, Lefebvre & Jarvis (2024, Nature Ecology & Evolution 8:806–816): 203 birds from 15 passerine species (13 wild-caught) on a cognitive battery; species better at extractive foraging problems (e.g., removing a lid) had higher field-reported technical innovation rates and larger brains; associative learning, reversal learning and self-control were unrelated to problem solving, wild innovation or brain size. The authors conclude novel motor solutions matter more than self-control or cue learning for technical innovation. — [Audet et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41559-024-02342-7); [McGill press release](https://www.mcgill.ca/newsroom/channels/news/model-evolution-intelligence-355739)
- New Caledonian crows (foundational): Gruber, Schiestl, Boeckle, Frohnwieser, Miller, Gray, Clayton & Taylor (2019, Current Biology 29:686–692) gave crows metatool problems with stages hidden from one another; crows avoided distractor apparatuses and non-functional tools, keeping track of out-of-sight tools and apparatuses while planning a sequence, described as "the first conclusive evidence that birds can plan several moves ahead while using tools." — [Gruber et al. 2019, PMC6384166](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC6384166)
- Earlier Auckland work: crows with experience of three component behaviours linked them into a novel sequence to solve a three-stage metatool problem on the first trial. — [University of Auckland repository](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/handle/2292/13166)
- Taylor & Johnston (2024, Current Biology 34:R996–R999) review physical-world understanding across capuchins, dolphins (sponge tools), crows, octopuses (coconut shells), tuskfish and string-pulling bees, arguing these may reflect cognitive understanding of the physical world. — [Taylor & Johnston 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2024.07.005)
- Innovation compensating for disability: Grabham et al. (2026, Current Biology 36:R320–R321) report a captive kea missing his entire upper beak who was undefeated in dominance contests and had the lowest stress-hormone metabolites in his group, credited to a novel "beak-jousting" technique described as a behavioural innovation. — [Grabham et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2026.03.004)
- Bastos, Claessens, Nelson, Welch, Atkinson & Taylor (2025, iScience 28:112156) used YouTube videos to document self-care tool use in 17 new parrot species, raising known tool-using parrots from 11 (3%) to 28 (7%); phylogenetic modeling suggests 11–17% of parrot species may be capable, linked to relative brain size and feeding generalism. — [Bastos et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2025.112156)
- Neophobia at scale: the ManyBirds Project (Miller et al. 2025, PLOS Biology 23:e3003394; 129 collaborators, 77 institutions) tested 1,439 birds from 136 species in 25 orders; dietary specialists and migratory species were more neophobic than generalists and non-migrants. — [ManyBirds 2025](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3003394)
- Self-control is not the bottleneck: Miller et al. (2023, PLOS ONE 18:e0289197) found New Caledonian crows chose delayed better rewards regardless of social context, whereas Eurasian jays shifted to immediate rewards when conspecifics were present. — [Miller et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0289197)
- Chimpanzees: van Leeuwen, DeTroy, Haun & Call (2024, Nature Human Behaviour 8:891–902) trained chimpanzees in two groups (66 total) on a sequential puzzle box; network-based diffusion analysis showed 14 naive chimpanzees learned to operate it after seeing trained conspecifics, despite failing to innovate it during three months of prior access to all materials; social learning was necessary and sufficient after the initial innovation. — [van Leeuwen et al. 2024, PMC11132989](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC11132989)
- Gunasekaram et al. (2024, Science 386:920–925): comparing migration-based genetic networks with shared-cultural-trait networks across the four chimpanzee subspecies, limited between-group connectivity favored the emergence of a few cases of chimpanzee cumulative culture, which likely developed via transmission between populations, incremental change and repurposing; increased mobility in Homo is proposed to have created lasting dependence on cultural exchange. — [Gunasekaram et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adk3381)
- Ohlinger, Call, Charbonneau & Sasaki (2026, TREE 41:527–540) argue existing animal experiments may lack conditions needed to detect cumulative culture even in capable species and propose a changing-environment paradigm illustrated with Temnothorax ants. — [Ohlinger et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tree.2026.03.003)
- Octopus: Dissegna, Borrelli, Ponte, Chiandetti & Fiorito (2023, Biology 12:1487) found more neophilic Octopus vulgaris approached a puzzle box sooner and were more likely to solve it, but the most neophilic were not the fastest solvers. — [Dissegna et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/biology12121487)
- Cetaceans: Hill et al. (2022, Animal Cognition 25:1091–1108) trained and tested nine killer whales (ages 5–29) on an "innovate" task across two facilities; animals showed high fluency, originality, elaboration and flexibility, with younger animals producing more variable behaviors than older ones. — [Hill et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-022-01635-3)
- Curiosity comparisons (see also Question 1): children explore uncertain options earlier than great apes (Sánchez-Amaro & Rossano 2023). — [Sánchez-Amaro & Rossano 2023](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0285946)

### Inferences
- The Audet et al. dissociation (motor problem-solving predicts innovation; self-control and cue learning do not) matches the human developmental picture: generating novelty is about producing varied actions on the physical world, not about inhibitory control.
- Younger killer whales' greater behavioral variability on an innovation task parallels the human "high temperature" childhood, suggesting age-related cooling of behavioral variability may be a general feature of long-lived, slow-developing species, though this is a single small study.
- The chimpanzee results isolate the human difference as accumulation rather than invention: individual innovation exists in many lineages, but without connectivity and reliable social transmission it does not compound.

### Gaps
- No 2023–2026 New Caledonian crow study specifically on tool innovation (as opposed to planning, delayed gratification or reviews) was retrieved; the metatool planning evidence is from 2019.
- Sample sizes and success rates for Gruber et al. 2019 are in the full text (PMC6384166), not retrieved here.
- Octopus and cetacean innovation evidence remains thin and small-N; no 2024–2026 cetacean innovation study was found.

## Key Question 6: Education and expertise (domain knowledge and idea generation across development; inverted-U; teaching transferable thinking skills)

### Takeaway
Recent evidence supports domain-specific rather than inverted-U effects of expertise: mathematics experts show higher mathematical but not general creativity, and expertise helps in evaluating creative products. Training evidence is sobering: creativity training's effect shrinks from 0.53 SD to about 0.3 SD after publication-bias correction, chess/music/working-memory training show small effects that fall with design quality, pretend-play training does not transfer to cognitive control, and most "critical thinking" meta-analyses come from professional education with high heterogeneity and no transfer tests.

### Cited Findings
- Meier, Gross, Vogel & Grabner (2023, Scientific Reports 13:12500): 115 mathematics experts vs 109 matched novices; experts had higher mathematical creativity and better short-term memory for structured domain material but did not differ on domain-general creativity. — [Meier et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-39309-w)
- Mutter & Hübner (2024, Scientific Reports 14:13675): across two experiments on composing and rating visual compositions, expertise did not change beauty judgments but only experts could predict a creator's creativity from the product. — [Mutter & Hübner 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-64494-7)
- DiStefano et al. (PsyArXiv 2025): engineering students (N = 99) and psychology students (N = 212) revised solutions after evaluating GPT-3.5 ideas; engineers produced more original and effective solutions, and both groups improved by comparable amounts ("rising tides lift all boats"). — [DiStefano et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/k2u87_v1)
- Simonton's theoretical update (2024, Physics of Life Reviews 51): combinatorial creativity as blind-variation and selective-retention, with an epistemology-based typology of rational, irrational and blind variations, of which only blind variations are "logically associated with creativity and knowledge production"; Simonton (2021, Frontiers in Psychology 12) defines personal creativity as the product of originality, utility and surprise and models discovery as combinatorial. — [Simonton 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.plrev.2024.09.008); [Simonton 2021](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.721104)
- Creativity training: Sio & Lortie-Forgues (2024, Psychological Bulletin), k = 169 studies, 844 effect sizes including 48 unpublished; unadjusted effect 0.53 SD, 0.29–0.32 SD after correcting for strong publication bias; high prevalence of methodological shortcomings. — [Sio & Lortie-Forgues 2024](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000432)
- Foundational far-transfer skepticism: Sala & Gobet (2017, Current Directions 26:515–520) report three meta-analyses (chess, music, working-memory training) showing small-to-moderate effects on children's cognitive and academic skills that shrink as design quality (active controls) rises. — [Sala & Gobet 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721417712760)
- Critical-thinking instruction meta-analyses 2022–2026 are dominated by professional education with large but heterogeneous effects and no transfer tests: inquiry-based learning g = 1.34 [1.06, 1.61], I² = 92%, k = 57, with control-group type a significant moderator (Harapan et al. 2026, F1000Research); PBL in nursing SMD = 0.47 [0.33, 0.61], k = 19, N = 1,996 (Wei et al. 2024); PBL in first-year medical students no significant effect (k = 7, N = 622; Manuaba et al. 2022); escape rooms SMD = 0.91 [0.50, 1.33], k = 20, N = 2,215 (Yang et al. 2025). — [Harapan et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.180569.3); [Wei et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1097/NNE.0000000000001548); [Manuaba et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0277339); [Yang et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nepr.2025.104508)
- Developmentally targeted interventions: Park et al. (2025, npj Science of Learning) preregistered RCT assigning 103 children aged 5–7 to question-asking vs careful-listening across eight science lessons over two weeks to foster curiosity (outcomes truncated in the record). Pretend-play training did not transfer to cognitive control (Adam et al. 2022; see Question 1). — [Park et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41539-025-00384-5); [Adam et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2021.105348)
- Children's divergent thinking relates to analytic reasoning style: Giancola et al. (2022, Cognitive Processing; 106 Italian children, mean age 8.6) found probabilistic reasoning on the Beads Task predicted Alternate Uses output, with slow effortful reasoning linked to higher divergence; Giancola et al. (2024, Children; 101 children, mean age 8.0) found field-independence mediated fluid-intelligence effects on divergent thinking. — [Giancola et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10339-022-01104-2); [Giancola et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/children11010089)
- Media multitasking: Shan et al. (2025, PsyCh Journal; 609 junior-high students, two waves six months apart) found earlier media multitasking predicted later lower divergent thinking (β = −0.10), partly mediated by executive function. — [Shan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1002/pchj.70038)

### Inferences
- The developmental and expertise literatures together suggest a trade: knowledge raises the quality and domain-relevance of ideas (experts' mathematical creativity; engineers' better solutions) but narrows the search (Park et al.'s narrowing of prior-knowledge use at population scale; adults' lower temperature and generalization). No 2023–2026 study directly tested an inverted-U, so the "too much knowledge hurts" leg rests on the population-scale bibliometrics and the child-vs-adult search data rather than on expertise studies.
- Teaching "thinking skills" shows little evidence of far transfer once design quality is controlled; interventions that change the informational environment (question-asking, external records, exposure to child-like evidence) have clearer mechanistic rationale than skill-drilling.

### Gaps
- No 2020–2026 empirical test of the inverted-U between expertise and creativity (Simonton/Weisberg debate) was located in Europe PMC; the retrieved expertise studies are group comparisons that cannot detect a turning point.
- No 2023–2026 meta-analysis of thinking-skills instruction in children with transfer outcomes was found; the available meta-analyses are in nursing/medical education.
- Outcome results for the Park et al. 2025 question-asking RCT were truncated in the record.
