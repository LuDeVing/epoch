## Key question 1: Dual-process theory in the 2020s — when and how does deliberation change an initial answer, and what triggers it?

### Takeaway
The field has moved from "intuition errs, deliberation corrects" to a non-exclusive model in which several intuitions (including "logical" ones) are activated at once; deliberation is triggered by an uncertainty/conflict signal or a low feeling of rightness, and across tasks (logic, risk, morality, insight, Moses illusions, fluid intelligence) most final correct answers were already correct at the intuitive stage, so deliberation changes answers less often than classical theory assumed — and when it does, it often fails for the most biased reasoners.

### Cited Findings

**The 2022/2023 BBS target article and commentaries**
- De Neys, "Advancing theorizing about fast-and-slow thinking", Behavioral and Brain Sciences 46, e111 (online Sept 2022; print 2023). Core claims (from abstract): dual-process models have assumed *exclusivity* (intuition and deliberation each generate responses the other cannot) and a *switch* mechanism; De Neys argues "there is no solid ground for such exclusivity" and that leading switch accounts are conceptually problematic because they presuppose exclusivity; he sketches a more viable architecture and research agenda. — [Cambridge/BBS](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0140525X2200142X/type/journal_article); [Europe PMC record, DOI 10.1017/S0140525X2200142X](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22Advancing%20theorizing%20about%20fast-and-slow%20thinking%22&format=json&resultType=core)
- The working model's switch rule, as quoted by commentators: in De Neys's model the switch "is considered to depend entirely on the relative activation levels of competing intuitions and the mysterious 'deliberation threshold'", and "System 2 deliberation will extend for as long as the uncertainty remains above the threshold" (target article section 3.4). — [Ackerman & Morsanyi 2023 commentary PDF](https://ackerman.net.technion.ac.il/files/2023/06/Ackerman_Morsanyi_inpress_commentary.pdf)
- The author's reply, "Further advancing fast-and-slow theorizing" (BBS 46, 2023, DOI 10.1017/S0140525X23000559), responds to 34 commentaries, organized around generalizability of the findings, links with other models, necessary extensions, the utility of dual-process models, and specific points. — [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/abs/further-advancing-fastandslow-theorizing/57847E353F098DC7325B3F7D6DFB3A1B)
- Commentary themes: Ackerman & Morsanyi (BBS 46, e112) argue the model omits meta-reasoning monitoring (confidence) and control (rethinking) and ignores when people stop deliberating or opt out ("under substantial uncertainty people are doomed to think forever" in the De Neys model as stated); Newman & Thompson (e133, "Not feeling right about uncertainty monitoring") argue the switch model overlooks the meta-reasoning framework on when to start and stop analytic thinking; Low, Butterfill & Michael (e130) argue mindreading research shows abundant evidence for exclusivity; Ferreira & Huettig (e121) argue language-processing data support De Neys's critique; Braem, Held, Shenhav & Frömer (e115, "Learning how to reason and deciding when to decide") argue that detecting the need for further thought is a learned, context-sensitive intuitive process grounded in cognitive-control/conflict-monitoring research. — [Ackerman & Morsanyi PDF](https://ackerman.net.technion.ac.il/files/2023/06/Ackerman_Morsanyi_inpress_commentary.pdf); [Newman & Thompson DOI 10.1017/s0140525x22003089](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0140525x22003089); [Low et al. summary](https://www.butterfill.com/writing/low2023_view/); [Braem et al. PMC10597599](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10597599/)

**Foundational defaults and trigger accounts (for context)**
- Evans & Stanovich 2013 (Perspectives on Psychological Science 8:223–241): rapid Type 1 processes give default responses unless Type 2 intervenes; Type 2 supports hypothetical thinking and places heavy demands on working memory. (foundational) — [DOI 10.1177/1745691612460685](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691612460685)
- Pennycook, Fugelsang & Koehler 2015 three-stage model (Cognitive Psychology 80:34–72): stimuli cue Type 1 outputs; conflict must be detected; Type 2 then takes the form of rationalization or decoupling; conflict detection and decoupling are separable and detection sometimes fails. (foundational) — [DOI 10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.05.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2015.05.001)
- Thompson, Prowse Turner & Pennycook 2011 (Cognitive Psychology 63:107–140): the Feeling of Rightness (FOR) accompanying an initial answer predicts rethinking; four tasks (N = 60, 48, 128, 64); low FOR → longer rethinking and more answer changes; FOR is driven by answer fluency. (foundational) — [DOI 10.1016/j.cogpsych.2011.06.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2011.06.001)
- Thompson et al. 2013 (Cognition 128:237–251): across nine experiments, answer fluency predicted FOR, which predicted thinking time and answer changes; perceptual (dis)fluency had no effect on FOR, confidence or accuracy in the five experiments that tested it, except that disfluency raised CRT accuracy only among high-ability participants. (foundational) — [DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2012.09.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2012.09.012)
- Bago & De Neys 2017 "Fast logic?" (Cognition 158:90–109): two-response paradigm (immediate answer under deadline/load, then free final answer); correct logical responses were "frequently" the first, immediate response. (foundational) — [DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2016.10.014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2016.10.014)
- Bago & De Neys 2019 (JEP:General, moral dilemmas, four studies): in more than 70% of cases where people chose the utilitarian response after deliberation, it had already been given in the initial phase. — [DOI 10.1037/xge0000533](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000533)
- Bago, Bonnefon & De Neys 2021 (JEP:General, seven economic-game studies): both prosocial and selfish responses were predominantly made intuitively rather than after deliberate correction. — [DOI 10.1037/xge0000968](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000968)
- Bago & De Neys 2018 EEG (Neuropsychologia): conflict base-rate problems increased centro-parietal N2 and frontal P3, indicating early (intuitive-stage) conflict sensitivity. — [DOI 10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2018.07.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuropsychologia.2018.07.017)

**2023–2026 two-response and "smart intuition" findings**
- Voudouri, Białek & De Neys 2024 (Cognition 250): in risky choice, most participants were loss-averse both intuitively and after deliberation; those who picked the expected-value option after deliberating had mostly already chosen it intuitively; loss-averse participants often showed intuitive sensitivity to expected value via lower confidence. — [DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2024.105837](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2024.105837)
- Beucler & De Neys 2026 "Intuitive insight" (Cognition 271): Compound Remote Associates under a two-response paradigm; correct final answers were usually preceded by correct intuitive answers, so extended deliberation was often unnecessary; items with smaller semantic search spaces were solved intuitively more often. — [DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2025.106422](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2025.106422)
- Beucler, Voudouri & De Neys 2026 "Moses illusions, fast and slow" (JEP:LMC 52(7)): three two-response experiments; many participants avoided the illusion without deliberating; incorrect responders showed error sensitivity via lower confidence even with minimal deliberation. — [DOI 10.1037/xlm0001530](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0001530)
- Charbit, Boissin & De Neys 2026 preprint (fluid-intelligence matrices): most correct answers came from corrective deliberation, but over 20% of sound reasoners were consistently correct intuitively ("smart intuitors"). — [PsyArXiv DOI 10.31234/osf.io/wmc49_v2](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/wmc49_v2)
- Boissin et al. 2026 (Trends in Neuroscience and Education 44): ~12-year-olds under time pressure/load showed high intuitive accuracy on well-practised numerical tasks but low on the bat-and-ball, arguing against a general developmental System-1 deficit and for knowledge automatization as the driver of sound intuiting. — [DOI 10.1016/j.tine.2026.100295](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tine.2026.100295)
- Beucler, Purcell, Charles, De Neys & Desender 2026 preprint "Thinking in Vain": base-rate task (N = 151) — choices, confidence and RTs reflected a shared evidence signal overweighting stereotypes; a meta-analysis (N = 2,428) found that thinking longer did not substantially improve error correction among highly biased participants. — [PsyArXiv DOI 10.31234/osf.io/ze2ku_v2](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/ze2ku_v2)
- Beucler & De Neys 2026 preprint "Rhythm of Thought": LLM-scored think-aloud protocols; correct responses showed more justification, control and regulation than biased ones; lower confidence predicted further deliberation. — [PsyArXiv DOI 10.31234/osf.io/mf3s9_v1](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/mf3s9_v1)
- Purcell, Desender & De Neys 2026 preprint: four preregistered experiments (N ≈ 1,200); negative feedback lowered confidence and increased answer revisions and second-stage response times; feedback-driven confidence predicted revisions. — [PsyArXiv DOI 10.31234/osf.io/qxzae_v2](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/qxzae_v2)
- Beauvais & De Neys 2026 (Acta Psychologica 270): three studies rating bat-and-ball arguments under intuitive vs deliberate conditions; deliberation significantly improved discrimination of justification strength; arguments matching one's own answer were rated higher (myside bias). — [DOI 10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107725](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107725)
- Debiasing "System 1": Franiatte et al. 2024 (Psychologica Belgica 64(1), N = 147 French speakers) — training improved the initial intuitive stage; immediately after training most solved problems correctly without deliberation; Franiatte et al. 2025 (Acta Psychologica 258, six studies, three tasks) — animated-video training improved intuitive responses with effects lasting two months; Boissin et al. 2024 (Cognition 243) — short training improved base-rate reasoning among most Himba participants in Namibia, with benefit growing with schooling; Boissin et al. 2025 (Memory & Cognition 53(3)) — a logic-focused attentional manipulation modestly improved both intuitive and deliberate base-rate use, while a heuristic-focused manipulation had no discernible effect. — [PMC11025568](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11025568/); [DOI 10.1016/j.actpsy.2025.105131](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2025.105131); [DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2023.105681](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2023.105681); [DOI 10.3758/s13421-024-01625-5](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-024-01625-5)
- Thompson & Markovits 2025 "Fast reasoning and metacognition" (Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 32(4):1915–1921): 289 participants, belief–logic conflict syllogisms; FOR for intuitive answers predicted both whether and in which direction answers changed under no time constraint — low-FOR belief-based answers shifted toward validity, high-FOR ones shifted toward belief. — [DOI 10.3758/s13423-025-02662-0](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-025-02662-0)
- Markovits, Thompson & Bergeron 2026 (Memory & Cognition 54(6)): four studies; under severe time pressure reasoners more often judged invalid conclusions invalid when premises had many alternative antecedents, i.e., semantic activation serves as an intuitive cue. — [DOI 10.3758/s13421-026-01859-5](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-026-01859-5)
- Rouhi, Gasimova & Thompson 2026 preprint (N = 998, belief-bias syllogisms, fuzzy-trace framing): accuracy dropped only when WM load and a deadline were combined; confidence was lower on conflict problems and predicted answer changes regardless of constraints, but monitoring resolution was low even without constraints. — [PsyArXiv DOI 10.31234/osf.io/sv3kf_v1](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/sv3kf_v1)
- Lay theory of deliberation: De Neys & Raoelison 2025 (Communications Psychology 3:141), 13 studies (human Ns 184–482 per study; 1,625 in Studies 1–7) — deliberative reasoners were rated better than intuitive ones even with accuracy held constant (Study 1: F(1,238) = 348.6, ηp² = 0.59; d = 0.53–1.58 across accuracy conditions; replications d = 0.26–1.69); 76.8% bet on the highly accurate deliberator vs 21.6% on the intuitor; the preference survived time pressure/load (d = 0.37–1.72), so the authors conclude the "folk theory" is itself primarily intuitive; ChatGPT 3.5/4 reproduced it (d = 1.44–4.77). — [PMC12480527](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12480527/)

### Inferences
- The 2020s consensus among De Neys, Thompson and Pennycook-style accounts is that the *trigger* for deliberation is a metacognitive signal (uncertainty from competing intuitions, low FOR, low confidence, negative feedback), not the content of a "System 2" computation; the dispute is whether that signal is adequately modelled by activation differences alone (De Neys) or needs explicit monitoring/control rules and stopping criteria (Ackerman, Thompson).
- Across domains the modal pattern is "deliberation confirms what intuition already produced"; corrective deliberation does occur (fluid-intelligence matrices are the clearest case), but its yield is lowest precisely for the most biased reasoners, which limits "think harder" as a remedy.
- Debiasing works best by changing intuitions (automatizing the correct response) rather than by inducing more deliberation — consistent with non-exclusivity.

## Key question 2: Metacognition — monitoring thought, computing confidence, and judging the quality of one's own ideas

### Takeaway
Confidence is increasingly modelled as an inferential, second-order computation (a self-model over evidence), supported by a domain-general medial/lateral prefrontal–precuneus–insula network with anterior/lateral PFC for integrating local signals into global self-beliefs; in creative ideation, people's judgments of their own ideas correlate only ~0.1–0.3 with expert ratings, systematically under-rank their best idea, and are biased by fluency and serial position — yet accurate monitoring appears to be a necessary condition for creative performance.

### Cited Findings

**Computation and neural basis of confidence**
- Fleming 2024, "Metacognition and Confidence: A Review and Synthesis", Annual Review of Psychology 75:241–268: proposes bridging subpersonal uncertainty representations with personal-level metacognition by focusing on propositional confidence in one's own (hypothetical) decisions; such confidence depends on the observer's models of the world and of their own cognitive system, which can be more or less accurate — explaining why metacognitive judgments are inferential and can diverge from performance; integrates neural findings on uncertainty and performance monitoring and proposes where metacognitive interventions act. — [Annual Reviews DOI 10.1146/annurev-psych-022423-032425](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-022423-032425); [PsyArXiv preprint DOI 10.31234/osf.io/ge7tz](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/ge7tz)
- Fleming 2026, "Towards an integrative neuroscience of metacognition", Nature Reviews Neuroscience (ahead of print): self-evaluations arise from structured transformations of uncertainty; evidence accumulation yields local confidence signals and prefrontal areas integrate them into global self-beliefs, supporting metacognitive control across timescales; dysfunction links to disorders of reality monitoring and insight. — [DOI 10.1038/s41583-026-01081-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41583-026-01081-x)
- Vaccaro & Fleming 2018 ALE meta-analysis of 47 neuroimaging studies (Brain and Neuroscience Advances): a domain-general network — medial and lateral PFC, precuneus, insula — tracks confidence in self-performance; right anterior dorsolateral PFC preferentially for decision-making studies, bilateral parahippocampal cortex for memory; results on metacognitive *sensitivity* were inconclusive; vmPFC/anterior dmPFC overlap with mentalizing. (foundational) — [PMC6238228](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6238228/)
- Lund, Corrêa, Fardo, Fleming & Allen 2025 (JEP:LMC 51(10):1594–1605, preregistered): metacognitive bias and efficiency correlated across visual perception, episodic memory and semantic memory even after controlling cognitive ability, with up to 15–20% shared variance. — [DOI 10.1037/xlm0001462](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0001462)
- Kozyra, O'Neill & Fleming 2026 (Open Mind): Bayesian confidence becomes more sensitive to decision-supporting evidence as the number of unchosen alternatives grows (normalization over alternatives), reproducing positive-evidence biases; the same mechanism appears in CNNs. — [PMC13618752](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13618752/)
- Katyal & Fleming 2026 (Psychological Medicine 56, e17; N = 1,447): anxiety-linked underconfidence grew with longer metacognitive judgment times and involved accumulation of negatively biased evidence about one's ability; gender-related underconfidence involved only criterion shifts. — [PMC12885344](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12885344/)
- Herregods, Le Denmat, Vermeylen & Desender 2025 (Psychological Review): among five evidence-accumulation models of *when people stop accumulating evidence for a confidence judgment*, a "flexible confidence boundary" model with two slowly collapsing boundaries fit best; speed/accuracy instructions for the decision changed decision boundaries, and instructions to give fast vs careful confidence changed confidence boundaries. — [DOI 10.1037/rev0000603](https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000603)
- Rollwage, Loosen, Hauser, Moran, Dolan & Fleming 2020 (Nature Communications 11:2634; MEG): high confidence amplified post-decision processing of confirmatory evidence and suppressed disconfirmatory evidence — confidence gates evidence and makes changes of mind less likely. (foundational link between metacognition and confirmation bias) — [PMC7250867](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7250867/)
- Katyal & Fleming 2026 (Presse Médicale 55(4)): review extending drift-diffusion models past the choice to confidence, error detection, changes of mind and confidence-dependent confirmation bias in clinical decisions. — [DOI 10.1016/j.lpm.2026.104369](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lpm.2026.104369)
- Shea's recent work: Miyamoto, Rushworth & Shea 2023 (TiCS 27(5)) propose that frontopolar cortex tracks and evaluates alternative choices while anterior lateral PFC compares simulations of possible future scenarios — the machinery of "what if" thought experiments; Miyamoto et al. 2025 (Nature Communications 16) show people project their own metacognitive estimates onto partners, accurately for equally/less skilled partners but not more skilled ones, tracked by anterior lateral PFC; da Silva Castanheira, He, Shea & Fleming 2026 (eLife 14) show spatial attention determines which parts of a maze become available for planning (value-guided construal); Dijkstra, Fleming & Shea 2026 (Neuroscience of Consciousness) give a functional analysis of imagination–reality monitoring. Shea & Frith 2019 (TiCS 23:560–571) argue a functioning global workspace requires metacognitive components (foundational). — [DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2023.01.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2023.01.005); [PMC11747205](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11747205/); [PMC13349381](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13349381/); [PMC13492659](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13492659/); [DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2019.04.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2019.04.007)
- Dijkstra, von Rein, Kok & Fleming 2025 (Neuron 113(15)): reality judgments depend on combined strength of sensory activity from imagery or perception in fusiform gyrus; fluctuations predict imagery–perception confusions trial by trial. — [DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2025.05.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2025.05.015)

**Meta-reasoning (monitoring and control of reasoning)**
- Ackerman & Thompson 2017 (TiCS 21(8):607–617): Meta-Reasoning framework — monitoring appears as feelings of (un)certainty based on heuristic cues that are not always reliable; prior metacognition research focused on memory rather than reasoning. (foundational) — [DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2017.05.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2017.05.004)
- Ackerman, Binah-Pollak & Lauterman 2023 (Journal of Intelligence 11(9):171): adults from China, Israel and Western countries solving nonverbal geometric problems — the Western group showed the highest overconfidence and poorest resolution; the Chinese group invested the most time, had the best success rate but solved the fewest problems per minute. — [PMC10532471](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10532471/)
- Bercovich & Ackerman 2026 (Radiology 319(2)): 2,806 decisions by 28 radiologists and 50 non-radiologists; overall overconfidence 14.73%; radiologists had higher success (73.59% vs 64.71%) and better resolution (γ = 0.37 vs 0.23); confidence was calibrated on easy cases but overconfident (>40%) on hard ones, and longer deliberation predicted more mistakes (hard–easy effect). — [DOI 10.1148/radiol.252834](https://doi.org/10.1148/radiol.252834)
- Helm et al. 2026 (JAAD International 25:110–116): 68 participants rating 50 dermatology images; accuracy and confidence rose with experience; medical students significantly overconfident; 12% of melanomas dismissed across groups. — [PMC12966684](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12966684/)

**Metacognition of idea quality (creative forecasting / ideational evaluation)**

- Lebuda, Hofer, Rominger & Benedek 2024 (Scientific Reports 14:11883; Study 1 N = 425, Study 2 preregistered N = 317; Alternate Uses under "be creative"): self-assessment–performance correlations were small to moderate (~0.1–0.3); classical quartile analyses showed the Dunning–Kruger pattern, but Glejser tests and quadratic regression gave weak or non-replicating support (quadratic ΔR² = 0.01, p = .035 in Study 1; p = .702 in Study 2); relative self-judgments showed a below-average tendency; conclusion: people have limited insight into their creative performance, and the quartile pattern is largely regression to the mean. — [PMC11126607](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11126607/)

- Sidi, Torgovitsky, Soibelman, Miron-Spektor & Ackerman 2020 (Acta Psychologica 203): across three experiments people judged later ideas as more original than earlier ones (tracking the serial-order effect) but systematically *underestimated* the originality of their own ideas. - Skurnik, Ackerman & Kenett 2025 (BMC Psychology 13:824; N = 106): objective originality was linked to broadly connected semantic networks, whereas *self-judged* originality was linked to highly integrated, efficient networks; number of ideas predicted both. — [PMC12288250](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12288250/)

- Colombatto & Fleming 2026 (Communications Psychology 4:114; seven preregistered experiments, Ns 52–113): observers judged AI agents as more confident than humans given identical behaviour, driven by prior beliefs about capability. — [PMC13461344](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13461344/)

### Inferences
- The same architecture that explains confidence in perception (second-order inference over noisy first-order evidence, prefrontal integration) plausibly underlies feeling-of-rightness in reasoning and self-evaluation of ideas; the creativity data (r ≈ 0.1–0.3, under-ranking of the best idea, fluency/serial-position cues) are exactly what one expects if monitoring relies on heuristic cues rather than direct access to idea quality.
- Metacognitive accuracy for ideas improves with task exposure and abstraction (construal level) and with evaluating others rather than oneself, which suggests practical levers: generate, then step back (temporal/psychological distance) or externalise evaluation.
- Confidence does not only read out quality, it also *controls* search: it gates subsequent evidence (confirmation bias), sets stopping boundaries, and directs attention to candidate options.

### Gaps
- The full text of Fleming 2024 could not be fetched (UCL and Annual Reviews returned 403; the OSF file link uses an expiring signed URL that WebFetch cannot follow), so the specific neural division of monitoring vs control regions in that review is reported only at abstract level.
- Runco's "ideational evaluation" literature was not retrievable through Europe PMC; no primary Runco source is cited.
- No 2023–2026 study was found that reports a direct correlation between creators' forecasts of their own ideas and later real-world success beyond Berg's 2016/2019 work; this appears to be a genuine gap in the literature as indexed by PMC.

## Key question 3: Mental effort, the expected value of control, and when people stop thinking

### Takeaway
Mental effort is robustly aversive (meta-analysis of 170 studies) yet also valued and meaning-giving (the effort paradox); the EVC framework now includes learned efficacy, control-adjustment inertia costs, and affect-centred valuation, while stopping is governed by confidence criteria that decline with time (Diminishing Criterion Model, 3-stopping-rule model with opt-out) and by position-dependent thresholds in sequential search that people adapt to variance but under-adjust to longer horizons.

### Cited Findings

**Effort as cost and as value**
- David, Vassena & Bijleveld 2024, "The unpleasantness of thinking", Psychological Bulletin 150(9):1070–1093: meta-analysis of 170 studies (125 articles, 358 tasks, 4,670 participants, 29 countries, all using the NASA-TLX) found a strong positive association between mental effort and negative affect; of 15 moderators only one was significant (somewhat less aversive in Asian studies); effort was aversive across task types, populations and continents — supporting effort-as-cost theories. — [DOI 10.1037/bul0000443](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000443); [preprint DOI 10.31234/osf.io/m8zf6](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/m8zf6)
- Inzlicht, Shenhav & Olivola 2018, "The Effort Paradox" (TiCS 22(4):337–349): effort is costly yet can add value to outcomes, so people sometimes choose options because they require effort. (foundational) — [PMC6172040](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6172040/)
- Inzlicht, Campbell & Saunders 2024 preprint "Effort Paradox Redux": three candidate resolutions — the paradox is illusory, effort-seeking reduces to reward maximisation, or the paradox is adaptive. — [PsyArXiv DOI 10.31234/osf.io/4u682](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/4u682)
- Campbell, Depow, Agarwal & Inzlicht 2025 (Communications Psychology 3:112; five studies, N = 2,569): effortful activities were rated more meaningful but less enjoyable (Study 1, N = 1,145); meaning gains plateaued at higher effort; experience sampling (N = 260) found effortful leisure boosted meaning while preserving enjoyment. — [PMC12286852](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12286852/)
- Zohar & Inzlicht 2026 (JEP:General 155(4):1103–1119; 12 studies, N = 1,957, demand-selection task): people avoided effort in general; learning others chose the hard task increased effort, but learning others chose the easy task did not reduce effort below control. — [DOI 10.1037/xge0001904](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001904)
- Ileri-Tayar, Bugg & Kool 2026 (JEP:General 155(7):1726–1747; five experiments, Ns 50–130): participants avoided options requiring internal switching between control states and options with higher interference, trading the two off. — [DOI 10.1037/xge0001940](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001940)
- Bustos, Jiang & Kool 2025 (Cognitive Neurodynamics 19:114): people learned to adjust control to statistical regularities in demand when unrewarded, but performance-contingent reward "masked" this learning. — [PMC12274171](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12274171/)
- Embrey & Inzlicht 2025 (TiCS 29(12):1061–1062) debate whether metabolic costs are needed to explain cognitive fatigue (letter; keywords: opportunity costs). — [DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2025.08.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2025.08.001)
- Kool & Botvinick 2018, "Mental labour" (Nature Human Behaviour 2:899–908): review of how people decide when to invest mental effort and regulate its intensity via behavioural-economic and AI-inspired frameworks. (foundational) — [DOI 10.1038/s41562-018-0401-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0401-9)

- Wolff et al. (incl. **Allocating deliberation time and deciding to stop**
- Ackerman 2014, Diminishing Criterion Model (JEP:General 143(3):1349–1368; five experiments): people invest time until confidence meets a goal, but the goal declines with time, producing negative time–confidence correlations in problem solving even though confidence rises within a solution episode. (foundational) — [DOI 10.1037/a0035098](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035098)
- Ackerman & Morsanyi 2023 (BBS commentary): stopping deliberation is governed by (a) a confidence criterion that "dynamically drops as people deliberate longer, reflecting compromising on expected success" and (b) a per-item time limit beyond which people are reluctant to think further; people also opt out ("I don't know") or seek external help; contextual factors (instructions to reason logically, load, time pressure) and individual factors (thinking dispositions, ability, knowledge, anxiety) alter reasoning time and choice. — [commentary PDF](https://ackerman.net.technion.ac.il/files/2023/06/Ackerman_Morsanyi_inpress_commentary.pdf)

- Braem, Held, Shenhav & Frömer 2023 (BBS commentary): "deciding when to decide" relies on a learned, context-sensitive intuitive process, per cognitive-control and conflict-monitoring research. — [PMC10597599](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10597599/)
- Frömer, Callaway, Griffiths & Shenhav 2025 (Open Mind 9:791–813): a Bayesian sequential-sampling model in which expectations and confidence guide which options to learn about; attention and confidence raise choice probability for better-than-average options and lower it for worse ones (two experiments). — [PMC12240722](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12240722/)
- Leng, Frömer, Summe & Shenhav 2025 (Nature Human Behaviour 9(3):521–533; six studies, N = 565): framing a choice as *inclusive* (more than one option may be selected) reduced competition during evidence accumulation, making decisions faster and less conflicted, distinct from raising urgency. — [PMC13423591](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13423591/)
- Leng, Fengler, Shenhav & Frank 2026 (PLoS Computational Biology 22(8)): response omissions (no response before deadline) carry information about the decision process; modelling omission probability improves parameter recovery in sequential sampling models. — [PMC13521452](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13521452/)

**Optimal stopping in sequential search**
- Baumann, Singmann, Gershman & von Helversen 2020 (PNAS 117(23):12750–12755): the optimal rule is to accept the first option above a position-dependent threshold; people diverge from it; a *linear threshold* heuristic fit two studies better than existing models and predicted search across environments, with a third study generalising to a real-world problem. (foundational) — [PMC7293628](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7293628/)
- Baumann, Schlegelmilch & von Helversen 2023, "Adaptive behavior in optimal sequential search", JEP:General 152(3):657–692 (note: JEP:General, *not* Psychological Review as stated in the brief). Study 1 (205 recruited, 199 analysed, MTurk; ticket-price search with 10 options per sequence; prices from N(180,10) vs N(180,40)): participants adaptively scaled thresholds to outcome variance, "suggesting that the value of an option is perceived in relative rather than absolute terms". Study 2 (70 recruited; sequences of 5, 10 or 20 options): a longer horizon lowered the initial acceptance level "but less strongly than would be optimal", and for longer sequences participants adjusted the threshold over time more weakly than for shorter ones; the initial threshold parameter was highly stable across conditions and predictive of search length and performance, implying an additive effect of context on an individually stable aspiration level; nearly all participants were risk-seeking at the end of a sequence (final normalised thresholds below the 50th percentile). — [Bremen open-access accepted version, DOI 10.26092/elib/3162](https://media.suub.uni-bremen.de/bitstream/elib/8128/1/Baumann_Schlegelmilch_von-Helversen_Adaptive%20behavior%20in%20optimal%20sequential%20search_2023_accepted-version.pdf); [publisher DOI 10.1037/xge0001287](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001287)
- Follow-up: "Beyond risk preferences in sequential decision-making: How probability representation, sequential structure and choice perseverance bias optimal search", Cognition 254, 106001 (2025). — [Bremen group publications page](https://www.uni-bremen.de/en/general-psychology/publications)
- Simonsen & Madan 2025 preprint, "Knowing when to stop": reviews the Secretary, Burglar, Devil's Task and Game of Pigs problems and argues tasks with known optimal policies can reveal human deviations; simulations show the Secretary-problem optimal policy stabilises as set size grows. — [PsyArXiv DOI 10.31234/osf.io/k5b8j_v1](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/k5b8j_v1)

**Stopping in idea generation specifically**
- Serial-order effect (later ideas more original) is real but tiny: Barbot, Van den Noortgate & Said-Metwaly 2026 (Journal of Intelligence 14(6):100) found it in all AUT prompt × scoring conditions but it explained at most 3.5% of variance, with weak individual-level consistency of slopes (ICCs < 0.31). — [PMC13300940](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13300940/)
- Agnoli et al. 2020 (NeuroImage 207; n = 20): originality rose with ideational time while response rate fell; EEG alpha shifted from desynchronisation to synchronisation across the sequence. (foundational) — [DOI 10.1016/j.neuroimage.2019.116385](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2019.116385)
- Kleinkorres, Forthmann & Holling 2021 (Journal of Intelligence 9(1):3; N = 51): "be-creative" instructions produced fewer but more creative ideas than "be-fluent"; expected load interactions were not significant. (foundational) — [PMC7838919](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7838919/)

### Inferences
- Combining the DCM with the serial-order data: continuing to generate ideas after the confidence criterion is met yields only marginal originality gains per idea (≤3.5% variance), so the "stopping problem" in ideation is dominated by cost-of-effort and opportunity-cost considerations rather than by large expected quality gains — unless the task is one where the search space is large and early ideas are stereotyped.
- The sequential-search results (stable individual aspiration level, under-adjustment to long horizons, relative valuation) predict that people with a long ideation horizon will accept early "good enough" ideas too readily, and that reframing quality in relative terms (percentile of one's own distribution) changes the threshold.
- Shenhav's 2024–2026 updates (control-adjustment inertia, affective valuation) imply that switching between generative and evaluative modes is itself costly, which favours phased "generate then evaluate" strategies over rapid alternation.

### Gaps
- No 2023–2026 study was found that directly models *when people stop generating ideas* with an EVC- or DCM-style stopping rule; the inference above bridges separate literatures.
- Effect sizes for the David et al. 2024 meta-analysis are reported only as "strong positive" in the abstract; the numeric pooled estimate was not retrievable.

## Key question 4: Working memory and attention as the machinery of deliberate thought; WM and attention breadth in idea generation

### Takeaway
Current models converge on a focus of attention holding roughly 3–5 (Cowan: "about three") chunks embedded in activated long-term memory, with capacity limits reflecting interference and fleeting attention rather than a fixed store; meta-analyses show WM capacity relates only weakly to creativity overall (r ≈ .08–.19), more to convergent than divergent thinking, consistent with the "persistence" pathway, while the "leaky attention"/reduced latent inhibition account has mixed 2024–2025 support (one positive, one null).

### Cited Findings

**Capacity, chunking and attention**
- Cowan et al. 2024, "The Relation Between Attention and Memory", Annual Review of Psychology 75:183–214: embedded-processes model — working memory = activated LTM (aLTM) plus a focus of attention (FoA) limited to about 3–5 unassociated items or chunks; aLTM limited by time (under a minute for poorly encoded items) and interference; fits of Cowan et al. 2012 recognition data concluded "about three chunks could be retained, no matter whether they were unrelated words or multiword phrases", with incomplete chunks allowed; the capacity limit may reflect fleeting attention used to encode/consolidate rather than continuous holding (Rhodes & Cowan 2018); the central attention-based component is ~1 item and constant across childhood (Cowan et al. 2018); the authors judge Oberauer's two-basis account (one-item focus + mutual interference) "not as contradictory" to embedded processes as it appears. — [PMC12143923](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12143923/)
- Oberauer 2019, "Working Memory and Attention — A Conceptual Analysis and Review" (Journal of Cognition 2(1)): distinguishes attention-as-resource from attention-as-selection; of resource accounts only storage-and-processing is well supported; under selection, attention filters WM contents and focuses on one item, and WM guides control by holding perceptual templates and task sets; in the reply Oberauer argues attention need not be a limited resource. (foundational) — [PMC6688548](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6688548/); [reply PMC6688546](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6688546/)
- Thalmann, Souza & Oberauer 2019 (JEP:LMC 45(1):37–55; four experiments): chunking improved recall of both chunked and unchunked items (reduced load); the benefit required chunks with unique elements and chunks early in the list; a chunk is retrieved from LTM as a compact representation that frees capacity. (foundational) — [DOI 10.1037/xlm0000578](https://doi.org/10.1037/xlm0000578)

- Evans & Stanovich 2013: Type 2 processing "places heavy demands on working memory". (foundational) — [DOI 10.1177/1745691612460685](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691612460685)
- Rouhi et al. 2026 (N = 998): syllogistic accuracy fell only when WM load and a deadline were *combined*. — [PsyArXiv DOI 10.31234/osf.io/sv3kf_v1](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/sv3kf_v1)
- Dougherty & Hunter 2003 (Acta Psychologica 113:263–282): the number of alternatives retrieved in a likelihood task correlated positively with operation-span WMC, and probability-judgment subadditivity correlated negatively with WMC. (foundational; WM as bottleneck for the number of hypotheses held) — [DOI 10.1016/s0001-6918(03)00033-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0001-6918(03)00033-7)

### Inferences
- A ~3-chunk focus of attention is the hard constraint on *combining* ideas deliberately: only a handful of candidate elements can be co-active and bound at once, so combination either proceeds pairwise/serially or relies on chunked LTM representations (expertise) to pack more content into each slot.
- The small, convergent-leaning WM–creativity correlations fit the dual-pathway reading: WM buys persistence and evaluation (convergent, selection, refinement) rather than generative breadth; the bottleneck claim and the persistence claim are both partly right, operating on different sub-processes.
- Attention breadth evidence remains split; the most defensible 2025 statement is that cognitive (controlled) inhibition relates to divergent thinking more reliably than latent inhibition does.

### Gaps
- No 2023–2026 paper from Zabelina's lab specifically on leaky vs flexible attention surfaced in Europe PMC; the 2016 result stands as the primary source.
- No study was found that directly measures the number of idea-elements people can hold while combining them (WM load during conceptual combination).

## Key question 5: Hypothesis generation and testing

### Takeaway
Hypotheses are generated from memory into a small working-memory set (HyGene), with the number generated limited by WM capacity and cued by recent/serial data; testing is biased by positive-test and confidence-gated evidence selection, but 2024–2026 work shows a common individual-difference factor in confirmation bias, that "thinking in opposites" nearly doubles rule-discovery success, that motor learning proceeds by hypothesis testing, and that children search hypothesis space more broadly than adults.

### Cited Findings

**Generation (HyGene and its dynamics; foundational unless dated)**
- Thomas, Dougherty, Sprenger & Harbison 2008 (Psychological Review 115(1):155–185): HyGene — hypotheses are retrieved from long-term memory into a working-memory "set of leading contenders" and then drive probability judgment and hypothesis testing; simulations show generation errors propagate into judgment biases. — [DOI 10.1037/0033-295x.115.1.155](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295x.115.1.155)
- Sprenger et al. 2011 (Frontiers in Psychology 2:129; three experiments): divided attention during judgment increased subadditivity; divided attention during *encoding* increased later subadditivity, fully mediated by the number of hypotheses generated. — [PMC3120978](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3120978/)
- Lange, Thomas & Davelaar 2012 (Frontiers in Psychology 3:215): serial order and consistency of data, and elicitation timing, shape which hypotheses are retrieved; HyGene extended with dynamic data acquisition. — [PMC3386498](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3386498/)
- Lange et al. 2013 (PB&R 20(1):171–176): at a slow symptom-presentation rate (1,500 ms) diagnoses favoured later symptoms (recency); at a fast rate (180 ms) they favoured earlier ones (primacy), attributed to competitive WM dynamics. — [DOI 10.3758/s13423-012-0316-9](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-012-0316-9)
- Lange, Buttaccio, Davelaar & Thomas 2014 (Memory & Cognition 42(2):264–274): the MAC procedure detects which hypothesis is currently in WM; a two-stage pattern — brief heightened activation, then a lower-activation holding state. — [DOI 10.3758/s13421-013-0354-1](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-013-0354-1)
- Buttaccio, Lange, Thomas & Dougherty 2015 (Memory & Cognition 43(2):247–265): the first hypothesis generated into WM guides visual attention (fits eye-movement data at aggregate and individual levels). — [DOI 10.3758/s13421-014-0463-5](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-014-0463-5)
- Bonawitz, Walker, Abbott, Griffiths & Gopnik 2026 preprint: sequential guesses about a novel causal relation — adults showed stronger dependencies between successive guesses and a less diverse option set than preschoolers; framed as simulated annealing with a "hot" childhood search cooling into narrower adult search. — [PsyArXiv DOI 10.31234/osf.io/qpj6n_v2](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/qpj6n_v2)

**Testing, confirmation bias and alternatives**
- Branchini, Bianchi & Burro 2025 (Memory & Cognition 53(6)): a "think in opposites" prompt nearly doubled success on Wason's 2-4-6 rule-discovery task and produced more first-attempt discoveries, via less repetition of the same hypothesis and awareness of the ascending/descending dimension, not more triples tested. — [PMC12402026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12402026/)
- Berthet, Teovanović & de Gardelle 2024 (Scientific Reports 14; N = 200, nine tasks from Wason selection, 2-4-6 and interviewee paradigms): bias scores converged more across paradigms within a bias component than across tasks within a paradigm — a common factor partly underlies confirmation bias. — [PMC11561113](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11561113/)
- Rollwage et al. 2020: confidence gates post-decision evidence processing (neural confirmation bias). — [PMC7250867](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7250867/)
- Lenz, Zohrevand, Rassin & Verschuere 2026 (PLoS One 21(1); 293 German police officers, 2×2): verification vs falsification and suspect vs evidence focus did not change guilt ratings of an innocent suspect, but did shift the next investigative steps proposed toward evidence-based and falsification-oriented steps. — [PMC12820782](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12820782/)
- Kojima et al. 2025 (BMC Medical Education 25; 108 medical students): verbalising evidence *against* the first diagnostic hypothesis lowered confidence (5.01 → 5.20 → 4.98 on a 10-point scale; steps 1–2 p = .04, 2–3 p = .01) and changed the diagnosis in four misdiagnosis cases. — [PMC11863399](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11863399/)
- Ding, Niyogi, Taylor & Tsay 2026 (npj Science of Learning 11; N = 560, visuomotor rotation): learners generated and tested specific action–outcome hypotheses, finding solutions abruptly rather than converging gradually. — [PMC13346944](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13346944/)
- Fazio, Khediri & James 2026 (PLoS One 21(8); 16 interviews with postdoctoral chemists): researchers valued deductive hypothesis testing as a marker of good science yet found it impractical in their own work. — [PMC13497266](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13497266/)
- Piksa et al. 2024 (Frontiers in Public Health 12; N = 1,479): informing people about confirmation bias improved veracity discernment, mainly among the most vaccine-negative. — [PMC11518834](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11518834/)
- Lopez-Lopez et al. 2025 (Annals NYAS 1550): conceptual review arguing generative-AI search can amplify confirmation bias through query phrasing, preference for belief-consistent content, and resistance to inconsistent information. — [PMC12412720](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12412720/)

**Generation architecture: "generate a few then evaluate" vs iterative refinement**
- Morris, Phillips, Huang & Cushman 2021 (Psychological Science 32(11):1731–1746; nine experiments, N = 3,972): people use cached value estimates from past contexts to quickly propose a few candidate actions (even when those values are irrelevant now), and deliberate planning then evaluates only that small set with context-specific criteria — a hybrid "generate-then-evaluate" architecture. (foundational for this question) — [DOI 10.1177/09567976211005702](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976211005702)
- Phillips, Morris & Cushman 2019 (TiCS 23(12):1026–1040): "the possible actions considered by default are those that are both likely to occur and generally valuable"; this bias serves decision-making and shapes causal reasoning, planning and moral judgment. (foundational) — [DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2019.09.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2019.09.007)
- Beucler & De Neys 2026: in remote-associate problem solving, correct intuitive generation usually precedes the final answer, i.e., refinement by deliberation is rarely what produces the solution. — [DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2025.106422](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2025.106422)

### Inferences
- HyGene plus the WM-capacity findings imply the candidate set under active consideration is tiny (bounded by the ~3-chunk focus), so the *order* and *recency* of cues strongly determine which hypotheses ever get tested; interventions that force re-sampling (opposites, verbalising counter-evidence, falsification focus) work by changing what enters the set, not by improving testing per se.
- Confirmation bias is better described as a confidence-gated search policy than as a reasoning error: high confidence narrows the set and suppresses disconfirming input (Rollwage), which is adaptive when the first hypothesis is usually right but costly when early hypotheses are stereotyped.
- The dominant empirical architecture is "generate a few (cheaply, from cached value/likelihood), then evaluate those" rather than exhaustive generate-then-prune or continuous refinement; iterative refinement appears mainly as *re-generation* triggered by low confidence or negative feedback.

### Gaps
- Klayman & Ha's 1987 positive-test-strategy paper (Psychological Review 94:211–228) is not indexed in Europe PMC and was not fetched this session; it is cited here from memory as the foundational account that positive testing is often a rational heuristic (DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.94.2.211 — unverified this session).
- No 2023–2026 paper explicitly contrasting "generate-then-prune" with "iterative refinement" as named strategies in human ideation was found; the contrast is inferred from Morris et al. 2021 and the De Neys lab's two-response results.
- No 2023–2026 HyGene-specific empirical update by Dougherty or Thomas was found in Europe PMC.

## Key question 6: Mental models, probabilistic reasoning, counterfactuals and what possibilities come to mind first

### Takeaway
Mental-model theory now treats reasoning as imagining possibilities (with "could be true and could be false" truth values and a non-standard modal logic), counterfactual explanations convey more possibilities than causal ones, and a consistent 2019–2026 line shows that the possibilities that come to mind first are sampled from a distribution weighting both likelihood and value/normality — largely outside conscious control, developing by age 6–9, and used by observers to infer others' values.

### Cited Findings

**Mental models and possibilities (Johnson-Laird, Khemlani, Byrne)**
- Johnson-Laird & Ragni 2025, "Reasoning about possibilities: Modal logics, possible worlds, and mental models" (PB&R 32(1):52–79): four mental-model claims about possibilities — including that inconsistent premises yield no possibilities — conflict with four core features of standard modal logics. — [PMC11836090](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11836090/)
- Johnson-Laird, Byrne & Khemlani 2023 (PNAS 120(40); three online experiments, n = 208): verifications of disjunctions used truth values beyond true/false, such as "could be true and could be false", which involve counterfactual possibilities. — [PMC10556550](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10556550/)
- Quelhas, Rasga & Johnson-Laird 2024 (Cognitive Science 48(8); four experiments): inferences from quantified modal premises supported mental-model theory and not standard modal logic. — [DOI 10.1111/cogs.13485](https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.13485)
- Sklarek et al. 2023 preprint (four experiments): people reason about disjunctions with intuitive mental models, yielding systematic errors; they infer "possibly p" from "possibly p or possibly q". — [PsyArXiv DOI 10.31234/osf.io/dxz5h](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/dxz5h)
- Johnson-Laird 2024 (eNeuro 11(10)): seven case histories of errors in cognitive-science thinking and a "model method" based on mental models of possibilities claimed to improve accuracy and speed. — [PMC11464009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11464009/)
- Bio & Khemlani 2026 (Cognition 271; earlier preprint: eight preregistered studies, N = 484): a model-based theory of mental-state reasoning; generative tasks revealed a systematic "omniscience" error (attributing knowledge of a deductive consequence to someone who lacks it). — [DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2025.106328](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2025.106328)
- Modeltheory.org paper archive lists 2023–2026 items including 2023possibilities, 2024models-of-possibilities, 2024reasoning-about-possibilities, 2026possible-causes (filenames only). — [modeltheory.org/papers](https://www.modeltheory.org/papers/)

**Counterfactual reasoning as a source of alternatives (Byrne)**
- Byrne 2024 (Memory & Cognition 52(1):182–196; four experiments): people judge some impossible conditionals true or false depending on real-world knowledge, against probability-comparison accounts. — [PMC10806019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10806019/)
- Celar & Byrne 2023 (Memory & Cognition 51(7):1481–1496; four experiments, n = 731): counterfactual explanations of AI decisions were rated more helpful and improved participants' own decision accuracy more than causal explanations, consistent with counterfactuals conveying more possibilities. — [PMC10520145](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10520145/)
- Byrne 2026, "Inference and Imagination" (Topics in Cognitive Science 18(3)) and "Imaginative Thought" (Journal of Cognition 9(1):34): deductive inference depends on imagining possibilities; imagination drives moral judgment, causal explanation and counterfactual inference; counterfactual conditionals are represented with mental models. — [PMC13397060](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13397060/); [PMC13330857](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13330857/)
- Ragni, Byrne, Mevius & Johnson-Laird 2026 preprint (four experiments): factual and counterfactual assertions share the same language and mental models; no linguistic cue in English marks a sentence as counterfactual. — [PsyArXiv DOI 10.31234/osf.io/6kmdp_v1](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/6kmdp_v1)
- Bernhard, Cushman, Wright & Phillips 2025 (JEP:General 154(1):96–110; fMRI): judging whether an agent was forced vs chose freely (implicitly requiring alternatives) activated 14 clusters — prefrontal, parietal, temporal, cingulate and caudate — more than judging what actually happened, with activity tracking judgments trial by trial. — [DOI 10.1037/xge0001676](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001676)

**Which possibilities come to mind first, and why (Phillips, Cushman, Knobe, Bear)**
- Phillips & Cushman 2017 (PNAS 114(18):4649–4654; three studies): adults treat immoral and irrational events as impossible *by default* (implicit representations), distinct from explicit reasoning about possibility. (foundational) — [PMC5422784](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5422784/)
- Bear, Bensinger, Jara-Ettinger, Knobe & Cushman 2020 (Cognition 194; three experiments): the options that come to mind by default are samples from a distribution combining likelihood and desirability; the distribution of numbers that come to mind resembles the product of the statistical and prescriptive distributions. (foundational) — [DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2019.104057](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2019.104057)
- Bear, Knobe & Cushman 2022 preprint: value information still shapes normality judgments and what comes to mind even when presented *after* encoding. — [PsyArXiv DOI 10.31234/osf.io/v6u9p](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/v6u9p)
- Mills & Phillips 2023 (Cognition 240): category members in particular regions of an empirically derived feature space are more likely to come to mind, largely without conscious control. — [DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2023.105549](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2023.105549)
- Kalkstein, Hook, Hard & Walton 2023 (JPSP 124(6); five studies, total N = 5,488): counternormative behaviours come to mind less often and are rated less desirable; a field study found a laptop-use norm reduced multitasking over a term. — [DOI 10.1037/pspi0000412](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000412)
- Kominsky, Knobe & Bonawitz 2025 (Developmental Psychology; children N = 397, ages 4–9; adults N = 189): 6–9-year-olds were biased by prescriptive ideals in both "first thing that comes to mind" and random-sample predictions, whereas adults kept the two apart. — [DOI 10.1037/dev0001994](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0001994)
- Acierno, Kennedy, Cushman & Phillips 2025 (Cognition 264; four studies): people expect options that come to mind to be common and valuable, so when someone generates an unusual option observers infer that person especially values it (including in moral contexts) — a folk theory of option generation. — [DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2025.106238](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2025.106238)
- Castillo, León-Villagrá, Falbén, Chater & Sanborn 2026 (Cognition 274; two experiments): sequences generated "as they come to mind" deviate from randomness in nearly the same way as deliberate random generation, supporting a Local Sampling account over monitoring-based theories. — [DOI 10.1016/j.cognition.2026.106554](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2026.106554)
- Miyamoto, Rushworth & Shea 2023: frontopolar cortex tracks alternatives and anterior lateral PFC compares simulated future scenarios — a proposed neural substrate for considering possibilities. — [DOI 10.1016/j.tics.2023.01.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2023.01.005)

### Inferences
- The possibility-sampling results and HyGene converge: the first candidates in any generative task are drawn from a likelihood × value (normality) prior, which is efficient for everyday decisions but is exactly the prior that *suppresses* novel, low-probability or norm-violating ideas; creative generation therefore requires deliberately shifting the sampling distribution (e.g., "think in opposites", sparse cues, counterfactual framing), not just sampling longer.
- Counterfactual framing is a cheap, evidence-backed way to widen the set of alternatives considered (Celar & Byrne; Branchini et al.), and it engages the same alternative-tracking prefrontal machinery (Bernhard et al.; Miyamoto et al.).
- Mental-model theory's treatment of "possibilities" as the representational currency of reasoning gives a common vocabulary for deliberation (enumerate/flesh out models), hypothesis generation (retrieve candidate models) and creative generation (sample unusual models).
