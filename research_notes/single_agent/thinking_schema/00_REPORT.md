# How thinking should work on novel problems: evidence report

Verification note. Items marked [V] were confirmed by web search this session (title, venue, headline result). Items marked [M] are cited from memory (authors, year, venue are reliable; exact numbers should be re-checked before being quoted). I did not invent any reference. Where a number is uncertain, I say so.

## 1. Expert humans

- **Representation first.** Experts sort problems by deep structure, novices by surface features. Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1981), "Categorization and representation of physics problems by experts and novices", Cognitive Science 5 [M]. Representation change is the core of insight: Ohlsson (1992), "Information-processing explanations of insight and related phenomena" [M]. Metcalfe & Wiebe (1987), "Intuition in insight and noninsight problem solving", Memory & Cognition [M], found that "warmth" feelings predict success on analytic problems but not on insight problems. A confident first instinct is therefore informative on routine problems and uninformative on insight ones. This matches your failure (a): the right strategy depends on the problem class.
- **Analogy needs prompting and structure.** Gick & Holyoak (1980, 1983), "Analogical problem solving" and "Schema induction and analogical transfer", Cognitive Psychology [M]. On Duncker's radiation problem, spontaneous transfer from a studied analogue was low and jumped when a hint was given. Two analogues compared side by side produced a transferable schema. Schwartz & Bransford (1998), "A time for telling", Cognition and Instruction [M], found that contrasting cases improve learning of the structure.
- **Hypothesis generation and testing has known biases.** Klahr & Dunbar (1988), "Dual space search during scientific reasoning", Cognitive Science [M]: scientists search a hypothesis space and an experiment space. Wason (1960) and Klayman & Ha (1987), "Confirmation, disconfirmation and information in hypothesis testing", Psychological Review [M]: people mostly test positive instances. Counterexamples and discriminating tests carry more information than confirmations. This is the human analogue of your failure (b): examples that pass do not separate the candidate hypotheses.
- **Fixation, and when to switch.** Luchins (1942) Einstellung. Bilalic, McLeod & Gobet (2008), "Why good thoughts block better ones", Cognition [M]: chess experts kept using a familiar solution while believing they were searching for a better one, so expertise can cause fixation. Switching is triggered by an impasse, such as repeated failure of the current approach, not on a schedule. Ohlsson's theory says an impasse is what drives representation change.
- **Incubation.** Sio & Ormerod (2009), Psychological Bulletin 135(1), 94-120 [V]. Meta-analysis found a small positive effect. It was larger for divergent-thinking tasks, with longer preparation, and with low-demand filler tasks. Incubation after real preparation helps. It is not a substitute for effort.
- **Explaining and verbalising.** Chi et al. (1989), "Self-explanations", Cognitive Science [M]: good learners explain examples to themselves. Ericsson & Simon thinking-aloud work, with the meta-analysis Fox, Ericsson & Best (2011), Psychological Bulletin [M], found that concurrent verbalisation does not usually change performance on analytic tasks. Schooler, Ohlsson & Brooks (1993), "Thoughts beyond words", J. Exp. Psych: General [M], found that verbalising can hurt insight problems. Both findings are boundary conditions for your failure (c): written reasoning helps analytic work but may not help every task type.
- **Program-induction view of human learning.** Rule, Tenenbaum & Piantadosi (2020), "The child as hacker", Trends in Cognitive Sciences [M]. Human concept learning favours simple rules: Feldman (2000), Nature [M] (Boolean complexity predicts learning difficulty), and Tenenbaum (1999), the size principle [M].

## 2. Best LLM reasoning and program-synthesis systems

- **Chain of thought (CoT).** Wei et al. (2022), arXiv 2201.11903 [M]. Sprague et al. (2024), "To CoT or not to CoT?", arXiv 2409.12183 [M], found in a meta-analysis that gains are concentrated in math and symbolic reasoning and small elsewhere. Liu et al. (2024), "Mind your step (by step)", arXiv 2410.21333 [M], found CoT can reduce accuracy on some tasks. Both support (c): removing reasoning from a task that benefits from it hurts. Evidence that CoT helps on code and grid-transformation tasks is indirect.
- **Self-consistency and voting.** Wang et al. (2022), arXiv 2203.11171 [M]: about +18 points on GSM8K with PaLM-540B. It works when answers are short and comparable. Brown et al. (2024), "Large Language Monkeys", arXiv 2407.21787 [M]: coverage (any sample correct) rises roughly log-linearly with samples over four orders of magnitude, but majority vote and reward models plateau. The gap between coverage and selection is exactly your (b).
- **Generate then verify.** Greenblatt (2024), "Getting 50% (SoTA) on ARC-AGI with GPT-4o", Redwood Research blog [V]: about 8,000 Python programs sampled per task, filtered by the example pairs, a revision step that shows the model its own program's actual outputs, and about 50% on the public eval set. Accuracy rose roughly with log of samples. Cobbe et al. (2021) verifiers, arXiv 2110.14168 [M].
- **Execution feedback.** Chen et al. (2023), Self-Debug, arXiv 2304.05128 [M]: gains of roughly 2-12 points, with the largest gains when unit tests were available. Shinn et al. (2023), Reflexion, arXiv 2303.11366 [M]: HumanEval pass@1 reported about 91 versus about 80 for GPT-4 baseline. Huang et al. (2023), "LLMs cannot self-correct reasoning yet", arXiv 2310.01798 [M]: revision without external feedback often makes answers worse. Feedback must come from execution, not from the model's own opinion.
- **Hypothesis search on ARC.** Wang et al. (2023), "Hypothesis Search: Inductive Reasoning with Language Models", arXiv 2309.05660 [M]. The model writes natural-language hypotheses, then implements each as a program, and programs are checked against examples. Reported large gains over direct prompting on an ARC subset (I recall roughly 12% to 33% for the automatic version, higher with human hypothesis selection; verify before quoting). Natural-language hypotheses before code is the key design.
- **Induction versus transduction.** Li et al. (2024), "Is Programming by Example solved by LLMs?", arXiv 2406.08316 [M], and Li et al. (2024), "Combining Induction and Transduction for Abstract Reasoning", arXiv 2411.02272 [M]: programs (induction) and direct output prediction (transduction) solve different tasks. An ensemble beats either. This is evidence for choosing or mixing strategies per problem.
- **Test-time augmentation and training.** Akyurek et al. (2024), "The surprising effectiveness of test-time training for abstract reasoning", arXiv 2411.07279 [M]: about 6x improvement over the fine-tuned baseline and about 53% on public validation with an 8B model. ARC Prize 2024 winners, the ARChitects, scored 53.5% on the private set [V, arcprize.org/2024-results]. Franzen, Disselhoff & Hartmann (2025), "Product of Experts with LLMs: Boosting performance on ARC is a matter of perspective", ICML 2025, arXiv 2505.07859 [V]: 71.6% on the public eval set. The method generates candidates, then re-scores each under many augmentations (rotations, transposes, colour permutations) and combines the likelihoods. The ARC Prize 2024 technical report is Chollet et al., arXiv 2412.04604 [M]. I could not retrieve the ARC Prize 2025 report or winners during this session, so I make no claim about 2025 entries.
- **No pretraining, MDL.** Liao & Gu (2025), "ARC-AGI Without Pretraining" (CompressARC), arXiv 2512.06104 [V]: about 76K-parameter network trained only on the test puzzle, minimising description length, solves about 20% of ARC-AGI-1 eval. This shows a simplicity prior alone carries substantial signal.
- **Diversity versus depth.** Large Language Monkeys (above) shows breadth gives coverage. Snell et al. (2024), "Scaling LLM test-time compute optimally", arXiv 2408.03314 [M]: the best split between parallel sampling and sequential revision depends on problem difficulty, and per-problem adaptive allocation gave more than 4x efficiency over best-of-N. Wang et al. (2024), PlanSearch, arXiv 2409.03733 [M]: diversity in natural-language plans, not in code samples, raised pass@k. Diversity is only useful when it is diversity of idea, and only if a selector can find the good one.
- **Adaptive strategy selection.** Zhou et al. (2024), Self-Discover, arXiv 2402.03620 [M]: the model picks and composes reasoning modules per task, with gains over fixed CoT on BigBench-Hard. Yao et al. (2023), Tree of Thoughts, arXiv 2305.10601 [M]. Evidence is real but modest; I know of no study showing forced contrarian strategies help in general.
- **Surface form sensitivity (your failure d).** Sclar et al. (2023), arXiv 2310.11324 [M]: formatting changes alone move accuracy by tens of points. Mirzadeh et al. (2024), GSM-Symbolic, arXiv 2410.05229 [M]: renaming and number changes lower accuracy. I found no study directly showing abstract codes harm small models. Your measurement is the evidence; the literature is consistent with it.

## 3. Choosing among candidates that all fit the examples

- **Behavioural clustering.** AlphaCode: Li et al. (2022), Science / arXiv 2203.07814 [M]. Generate about a million programs, filter by examples, run the survivors on model-generated new inputs, cluster by identical outputs, submit one from each of the largest clusters. Clustering gave a clear gain at 10 submissions.
- **Agreement with generated tests.** CodeT: Chen et al. (2022), arXiv 2207.10397 [M]. Dual agreement between programs and model-written tests raised HumanEval pass@1 for code-davinci-002 from about 47% to about 66%. Weakness: the tests share the generator's blind spots.
- **Minimum Bayes risk.** Shi et al. (2022), "Natural language to code translation with execution", arXiv 2204.11454 [M]: choose the candidate whose execution outputs agree most with the others. This is the formal version of clustering.
- **Simplicity (Occam/MDL).** Human-learning evidence above [M]; CompressARC [V]; program-synthesis practice (FlashFill-style ranking, Gulwani 2011 [M]). Shorter or less-parameterised programs generalise better when examples are few. Pragmatic reasoning also matters: Shafto, Goodman & Griffiths (2014), Cognitive Psychology [M], show that example givers choose examples to be informative, so a rule that explains why these particular examples were chosen is more likely.
- **Agreement under augmentation.** Franzen et al. [V]: candidates that stay likely under rotations and colour permutations are better. This tests whether the rule is invariant to the symmetries the task does not break.
- **Strength of evidence.** Clustering and test agreement are well supported for code. Augmentation scoring is well supported on ARC. MDL is supported by one strong demonstration and by human data, but I know of no head-to-head against clustering on ARC. When several candidates pass all examples, the first-passing rule has no support.

## 4. Recommended schema

### One thinker, one novel problem
1. Read the problem in its original wording; restate it in your own words, naming inputs, outputs and what stays constant. (Strong: representation drives expert performance, Chi 1981; surface rewriting harms, Sclar 2023.)
2. Study every example pair and write down what changes and what does not, including contrasts between pairs. (Strong in humans: Schwartz & Bransford; Chi 1989.)
3. Write the reasoning in plain language before any code: 2 to 4 candidate rules, each one sentence. (Strong: CoT where tasks are analytic, Sprague 2024; Hypothesis Search. Weak for insight-like tasks.)
4. Take the first rule, check it by hand against all examples, and report each mismatch. If it fits, keep it and go to step 5; do not force doubt. If it fails, an impasse has occurred: change the representation, not just the details. (Moderate: Ohlsson 1992; Metcalfe & Wiebe 1987. Contradicts the always-doubt policy.)
5. Ask what else fits the same examples. Write one alternative rule and one input on which the two would differ. (Strong in principle: Klayman & Ha 1987. Weak that an LLM does this reliably.)
6. Implement as a program. Run it. Feed back actual outputs and the diff, not the model's opinion. Revise at most a few rounds. (Strong: Self-Debug, Reflexion, Greenblatt; self-critique without execution is not, Huang 2023.)
7. Prefer the simpler program among those that fit. Output the program, the rule, and the key assumption made. (Moderate: MDL.)

### Group of thinkers plus selector
1. Do not force a strategy on any thinker. Assign diversity of hypothesis (different first rules, or different plan sketches), keep each thinker free to drop the assignment if it does not fit. (Moderate: PlanSearch; Large Language Monkeys. Weak: no evidence for contrarian prompts.)
2. Mix induction (program) and transduction (direct output) across thinkers. (Moderate: Li 2024.)
3. Filter to candidates that pass all examples.
4. If more than one distinct behaviour survives, do not pick the first. Generate new inputs, including augmented versions of the examples (rotation, colour permutation, as far as the task allows), run all survivors, and cluster by output. (Strong for code: AlphaCode, CodeT, MBR; strong on ARC: Franzen 2025.)
5. Choose the largest cluster. Break ties by shortest or simplest program, then by likelihood under augmentation. (Moderate: MDL; augmentation scoring.)
6. If clusters split evenly and the inputs where they differ can be identified, submit multiple answers if the benchmark allows (ARC allows two attempts). Otherwise record the disagreement as uncertainty. (Moderate: AlphaCode's multi-submission.)
7. Adapt budget per problem: stop early when all thinkers agree, spend more when they split. (Moderate: Snell 2024.)

### Applied to your four failures
- (a) Remove mandatory doubt; make doubt conditional on a failed check (steps 4-5 above).
- (b) Replace first-pass selection with clustering on new inputs plus a simplicity tiebreak.
- (c) Require a written plain-language rule before code, but keep it short; the evidence says reasoning-free code hurts on analytic tasks.
- (d) Keep original wording. Abstraction can be applied to the selector's test inputs, not to the thinker's problem statement.

Caveat. Most LLM evidence comes from larger models and benchmark settings; behaviour of your small model may differ, so each step above should be treated as a hypothesis to measure in your harness, not as settled.
