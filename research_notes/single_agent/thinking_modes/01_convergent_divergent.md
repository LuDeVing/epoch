# Convergent vs divergent thinking, strategy choice, problem typing, dual process

Date: 2026-10-16. Tags: [V] = citation and gist checked via web search this session (usually abstracts or secondary summaries, not full text; noted where so); [M] = from memory, not checked. No full papers were read, so effect sizes are only quoted where a source stated them.

## 1. Convergent and divergent thinking, and how skilled thinkers combine them

- **Guilford (1950, "Creativity", American Psychologist; 1967, The Nature of Human Intelligence, McGraw-Hill)** [M]. Source of the divergent-production vs convergent-production distinction. Divergent = many varied answers; convergent = one best answer. Guilford treated both as abilities, not as a rule that one is better.
- **Cropley (2006), "In praise of convergent thinking", Creativity Research Journal 18(3), 391-404** [V, citation only; abstract not retrieved]. Cited by later work for the claim that convergent thinking is where evaluation and refinement happen, divergent is where variation happens, and real creative cognition needs both. Creative output must be effective, not just novel, which is a convergent test.
- **Finke, Ward & Smith (1992), Creative Cognition: Theory, Research, and Applications, MIT Press** [M; web search returned nothing relevant]. Geneplore: a generate phase builds "preinventive structures" (loose, ambiguous mental forms), then an explore phase interprets, tests and refines them. The cycle repeats, and constraints are applied during exploration. Evaluation is part of the creative process, not a separate stage.
- **Basadur, Graen & Green (1982), "Training in creative problem solving: Effects on ideation and problem finding and solving in an industrial research organization", Organizational Behavior and Human Performance 30** [V for the study and its content, via Basadur summaries; exact title from memory]. A diverge-then-converge ("ideation-evaluation") pair is applied inside each stage: problem finding, problem solving, implementation. The key device is deferred judgment, a strict separation of the two modes in time. The authors speculated that the best ideation-evaluation ratio differs by stage. A critique found in the search says strict sequencing is too simple and the two modes are interwoven in practice.
- **Nijstad, De Dreu, Rietzschel & Baas (2010), "The dual pathway to creativity model: Creative ideation as a function of flexibility and persistence", European Review of Social Psychology 21, 34-77** [V, via abstracts and secondary discussions]. Originality comes from either flexibility (many categories) or persistence (deep exploration of few). Positive mood goes with flexibility, negative mood with persistence. A secondary source reports flexibility correlates more with originality (r about .36) than persistence does (about .12). That figure is second-hand.
- **Jansson & Smith (1991), "Design fixation", Design Studies 12(1), 3-11** [V]. Designers shown an example solution copied its features, even when told not to. This is the source of the "avoid the obvious first idea" tactic in our system. The setting is design, where fixation limits output. It says nothing about problems with one correct answer.

**What this says about when divergence helps or hurts.** Divergence is useful when the target is novelty and the criterion is open. A single-answer puzzle has a fixed criterion, and the shortest consistent rule is the answer. Fixation research measures a loss of variety, not a loss of correctness. Our ARC failures are consistent with this, but I found no study that tests random-seed cards on single-answer tasks. That claim is inference, not evidence.

## 2. How people choose a strategy

- **Siegler (1996), Emerging Minds, Oxford UP** [M]. Overlapping waves: a person holds several strategies at once, and the mix shifts with experience. **Lemaire & Siegler (1995), "Four aspects of strategic change: Contributions to children's learning of multiplication", JEP: General 124(1), 83-97** [V, via secondary descriptions]. Strategy competence has four parts: repertoire, frequency, efficiency, and adaptivity (choosing the best strategy for the item). The paper uses a choice/no-choice method to separate how well a strategy runs from how well it is chosen. A later result from the search: low-knowledge students rarely switched from an incorrect strategy to a correct one, which the authors tie to weak understanding rather than weak recall.
- **Nelson & Narens (1990), "Metamemory: A theoretical framework and new findings", Psychology of Learning and Motivation 26** [M]. Object level and meta level, with monitoring up and control down.
- **Ackerman & Thompson (2017), "Meta-Reasoning: Monitoring and Control of Thinking and Reasoning", Trends in Cognitive Sciences 21(8), 607-617** [V]. Monitoring produces feelings of certainty from heuristic cues that are not necessarily reliable. Control uses them to continue, stop, or change strategy. Confidence is often poorly calibrated yet still governs effort.
- **Thompson, Prowse Turner & Pennycook (2011), "Intuition, reason, and metacognition", Cognitive Psychology 63, 107-140** [V, citation and gist via citing papers]. The Feeling of Rightness (FOR) attached to a first answer predicts whether people reconsider it. Easy, fluent answers raise FOR, and conflicting candidate answers lower it. FOR works as the trigger for slow analysis.
- **Metcalfe & Wiebe (1987), "Intuition in insight and noninsight problem solving", Memory & Cognition 15, 238-246** [V for gist, via secondary sources]. Warmth ratings rise gradually for step-by-step problems but stay cold then jump for insight problems. So in insight problems, apparent progress is a poor guide. A later study (Hedne, Norman & Metcalfe 2016) found no warmth difference with magic tricks, so the result is not uniform.
- **Ohlsson (1992), "Information-processing explanations of insight and related phenomena", in Keane & Gilhooly (eds.), Advances in the Psychology of Thinking, 1-44** [V, citation; contents from secondary sources]. An impasse is the point where the current problem space cannot yield a solution. Escape needs a changed representation. Knoblich et al. (1999, JEP:LMC) give mechanisms: constraint relaxation and chunk decomposition. Reaching an impasse does not guarantee insight.
- Cost of switching: I did not find a specific verified source on strategy-switch costs in problem solving. Treat as an open gap; the rules below treat switching as costly by design, not by evidence.

## 3. Classifying problems

- **Simon (1973), "The structure of ill-structured problems", Artificial Intelligence 4, 181-201** [V]. The boundary between well- and ill-structured problems is vague. A problem looks ill-structured partly because of the solver (large memory, external feedback). The same information-processing framework applies to both. Implication: problem type is a judgment about the solver's situation, so it should be a revisable estimate.
- **Reitman (1964/65), "Heuristic decision procedures, open constraints, and the structure of ill-defined problems", in Shelly & Bryan (eds.), Human Judgments and Optimality** [M]. Ill-defined problems have open constraints in start state, goal state, or operators.
- **Chi, Feltovich & Glaser (1981), "Categorization and representation of physics problems by experts and novices", Cognitive Science 5(2), 121-152** [V]. Experts sort problems by the principle needed; novices by surface features. Eight experts and eight novices sorted 24 problems. Implication: typing by deep structure ("is there a hidden rule to infer?") beats typing by surface form.

## 4. Dual process and when intuition is reliable

- **Evans & Stanovich (2013), "Dual-process theories of higher cognition: Advancing the debate", Perspectives on Psychological Science 8(3), 223-241** [V]. Type 1 processes (fast, autonomous) give default responses unless Type 2 (effortful, working-memory-based) intervenes. Defining features, not a monolithic "system".
- **Kahneman (2011), Thinking, Fast and Slow, FSG** [M].
- **Kahneman & Klein (2009), "Conditions for intuitive expertise: A failure to disagree", American Psychologist 64(6), 515-526, doi 10.1037/a0016755** [V]. Intuition is trustworthy when the environment is regular enough to be predictable and the person has had a chance to learn those regularities. Subjective confidence is not a dependable sign of accuracy. Link to ARC: the first reading was usually right (a regular, learnable task family, with evidence from our own runs), while confidence alone is not enough to certify it.

## Design rules

1. **Classify first, cheaply, and by deep structure, with a probability, not a label.** Ask: is there a verifiable ground truth or a single hidden rule? Are the goal, constraints and evaluation fixed? (Simon 1973; Reitman; Chi et al. 1981.) Output P(single-answer) and keep it revisable.
2. **Default to convergent on single-answer problems: take the simplest reading first, with no distorting cards.** Divergence is justified only by evidence of failure or by an open criterion. (Kahneman & Klein 2009; Jansson & Smith 1991 applies to design output, not single-answer tasks.)
3. **Make the first answer carry a confidence signal and use it as the switching trigger.** Use FOR-like cues: agreement among cheap independent samples, ease of derivation, and absence of competing candidates. High agreement means stop; disagreement or a failed check means escalate. (Thompson et al. 2011; Ackerman & Thompson 2017.) Do not trust self-reported confidence alone, so prefer external checks such as running the candidate rule against all examples. (Kahneman & Klein 2009.)
4. **Escalate in graded steps, not a mode flip.** Order: re-check, resample the same reading, re-read the problem (alternative representation), only then use strong divergence cards. This matches Siegler's overlapping repertoire and adaptive choice, and keeps switching cost low. (Lemaire & Siegler 1995.)
5. **Treat repeated failure with no verified candidate as an impasse, and respond with a representation change, not more of the same.** Impasse cues: all samples fail verification, or candidates repeat the same error. Response: relax a constraint or re-chunk the input (Ohlsson 1992; Knoblich et al. 1999). Do not use "feels close" as progress evidence on insight-like problems. (Metcalfe & Wiebe 1987.)
6. **On open-ended problems, run diverge-then-converge cycles with deferred judgment.** Generate under no evaluation, then select against explicit criteria, and repeat; explore each generated structure before discarding it. (Basadur 1982; Finke, Ward & Smith 1992; Cropley 2006.) Spend the convergent budget: a divergent pool without evaluation is unfinished work.
7. **Within divergence, deliberately vary breadth and depth.** Run some branches that cover many categories (seed cards, assumption flips) and some that go deep in one. (Nijstad et al. 2010.)
8. **Keep the two modes as separate arms with a router, and log per-class results.** Track accuracy by problem class (single-answer vs open) and by card type so the router's prior is learned from evidence. Our ARC result (2-4x worse with cards) is the first data point; the router should be tested on mixed sets. Not from a cited study; this is a validation step.
9. **Re-type during the run.** If a problem classified single-answer shows several equally verified answers, or one classified open turns out to have a checkable target, update the class. (Simon 1973: type depends on the solver's situation.)

## Gaps

- Switching-cost evidence for problem-solving strategies was not found.
- Geneplore, Guilford, Siegler 1996, Nelson & Narens, Reitman, Kahneman 2011 are [M] only.
- Nothing found that directly tests random-stimulus or assumption-reversal prompts on single-answer problems; our ARC data is the only evidence.
